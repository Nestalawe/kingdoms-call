Part One · The Basics

The Game at a Glance

Kingdoms Call is a slow-burn fantasy grand-strategy game played by email. Each of you rules a kingdom on a shared, hidden map. Every round you write a set of orders for your king and heroes; when every player has sent theirs, the world resolves them all at once and returns a private chronicle of what your people saw and did. Then you write the next round.

You expand by exploring the fog-shrouded map, conquering the neutral provinces that fill it, and clearing the monster lairs and ruins hidden in the wild. You grow stronger by recruiting armies, training your heroes, and hoarding gold — while watching the rival kingdoms doing exactly the same.

There is no fixed number of turns. The game ends at the Reckoning: when one kingdom (or an allied bloc) comes to dominate the world, its king may lay claim to the vacant Imperial throne. The claimant is then judged against every rival across eight Imperial Titles, and the kingdom with the greatest legacy is crowned. Full detail below.

The whole game in one sentenceGrow faster than your neighbours, avoid ruinous wars until you're ready, and be the dominant power when the music stops.

How a Turn Works

A turn represents a season. Within it, time passes in five phases. You give each character a list of up to five orders — one per phase — and they carry them out in order, phase 1 through phase 5.

Inside a phase

Every character in the world acts in the same phase before the next phase begins. Movement, battles, spells, recruiting and everything else for phase 1 resolve, then phase 2, and so on. This is why when you do something matters as much as what you do: a hero who moves in phase 1 can be somewhere useful by phase 3; a spell cast in phase 2 can shape a battle in phase 4.

Order matters — dependency and initiative

The engine resolves interacting orders in a sensible sequence (for instance, a hero must arrive in a province before they can fight for it), and clashing actions are decided by the characters' relevant skills. You don't manage this yourself — you just need to know that a plan spread sensibly across the phases beats one crammed into a single phase.

End of turn

After phase 5, the season closes: tax is collected and wages are paid (in that combined step), diplomacy declarations take effect, titles are re-awarded, and temporary effects expire. Then your chronicle is written and the next turn opens.

RememberProvinces you conquer during a turn are counted before that turn's tax is collected — so a province taken in phase 3 pays you at the end of the very same turn. Conquest can fund itself within the turn.

Founding Your Kingdom

Before the game begins you shape your kingdom with four choices.

Race

Human, Elven, Dwarven or Orcish. Your race sets the flavour of your home troops, the units you'll raise cheaply at home, and a distinct set of racial gifts — from the woodland-fighting of the elves to the mithril of the dwarves. It is not a straitjacket: you can lead other races with the Druid skill, and troops you recruit take the race of the province they're raised in, not yours. Race is important enough to have its own chapter — see The Four Races.

Alignment

One of seven moral stances — Divine, Good, Druidic, Neutral, Pagan, Evil, Undead — running from holy to unholy. Alignment decides your fanaticism in battle, which heroes you can afford to hire, and your kingdom's unique signature action. This is the single most flavour-defining choice; see The Seven Alignments.

Your king's temper

Berserk, Brave, Cautious or Cowardly — how your king behaves in personal combat. Every temper is viable; see Personal Combat & Temper. A Cowardly king is a poor duellist but the safest to keep alive, which matters, because losing your king is catastrophic.

Your king's skills

You distribute a pool of starting skill points across the fifteen skills. This is your opening strategy in miniature: points in Sage start the long game of kingdom-wide discoveries; points in Tactical or Druid make your armies hit harder; points in a magic school put spells in your hand from turn one.

What you start with

You begin with your king, a small starting army, two heroes, a modest purse of gold, and a single home province — the richest tile you'll be handed, fully explored, and yours. Everything else on the map you must go and take.

Part One · Blood & Kin

The Four Races

Four peoples contest the vacant throne, and each fights the world in its own way. Where alignment is the soul of your kingdom, race is its blood — it colours the troops you raise at home, the ground you were born to, and a handful of gifts no other people share. Two of those gifts belong to every race alike — the pull of kinship and the weight of old grudges — and the rest are each people's own.

Kinship & the banner (all races)

Soldiers fight harder among their own kind and under a lord of their own blood. Two separate bonuses reward keeping your kin together, and they stack:

Racial affinity — both bonuses apply to a unit's base strength
BonusWhen it appliesEffect
The King's kinThe unit is of the same race as your king (your chosen race).+20%
The Leader's kinThe unit is led by a character of its own race (king or hero).+20%

A king personally leading troops of his own race therefore fields them at ×1.44 — a powerful reason to keep your monarch at the head of a home-blood army. Because recruits take the race of the province they're raised in, a conquered foreign province gives you foreign troops: strong soldiers, but strangers to your crown who earn no King's-kin bonus until you lead them with one of their own.

Ancient grudges

Two feuds are older than any kingdom. Elves and Dwarves will not serve one another gladly, and neither will Humans and Orcs. An army led by a commander of its rival race fights at −20%. Leading captured elven bowmen with a dwarven hero is possible — but they will resent every order.

The peoples

Each race is born to a signature terrain (where most of its provinces are found) and carries two gifts of its own, which apply to that people serving a kingdom of their own race.

🏰Human — the Adaptable

Home ground: plains. Humans hold no single mastery but learn faster than anyone — the race that rises by effort rather than birthright. Their armies field balanced foot, cavalry and the versatile Free Lances.

  • Drillmasters — human units improve their experience level twice as readily, both from a commander's drills and from surviving battle.
  • Quick learners — human characters gain skill levels 50% faster, in every skill and by every means.

🌲Elven — the Woodland Folk

Home ground: forest. Elves are archers and scouts without peer, deadliest among the trees and swift to chart the wild places. Their rosters lean on bowmen, wardens and eagle-riders.

  • Woodland mastery — elven armies fight at +20% in forest and jungle.
  • Pathfinders — elven characters explore forest and jungle twice as fast, whether marching in or exploring.

⛰️Dwarven — the Mountain Kin

Home ground: mountain. Dwarves are the great defenders and finest smiths, unbreakable behind a shield wall and clad in armour no other race can forge. Their line is heavy foot, crossbows and the mighty Mountain Guns.

  • Bulwark — when defending, dwarven armies fight at ×1.5, on top of the usual defender's edge — a dug-in dwarf hold is a nightmare to storm.
  • Mithril Armour — every dwarven character bears a suit of mithril (granted at the game's start, and to any dwarf hired into the kingdom). Melee blows against the wearer do 2 less damage.

🔥Orcish — the War Horde

Home ground: desert. Orcs live for the attack and grow stronger the longer the war runs. They swarm with berserkers, warg-riders, goblin hordes and wyverns, and pay for their wars with plunder.

  • War-spoils — orcish units that win a battle as part of an attacking force plunder the field: they cost only half wages that turn.
  • Blood-vigour — orcish characters harden with every year of war, their maximum health rising by 2–3 each turn (growth, not healing — the fresh vigour is filled in by rest).
Choosing a raceRace and alignment are chosen separately, so any pairing is legal — but some sing. Dwarven + Good makes an immovable defensive wall; Orcish + Evil is relentless aggression that pays for itself; Elven + Druidic rules the deep woods; Human + anything leans on the fastest heroes and troops in the game. Play to your blood: keep your kin under a lord of their own race, and fight on the ground you were born to.

Part Two · The World

The World & Provinces

The map is a grid of provinces, most of it hidden under fog of war at the start. You see only what your characters have visited or scouted; everything else is guesswork until you send someone to look. Intelligence you gather (and steal) persists across turns.

Terrain

Provinces are plains, forest, jungle, hill, mountain, desert or sea. Terrain shapes which troops thrive there and which suffer — cavalry flounder in forest, missile troops falter at sea, and mountains squeeze a battle line so numbers count for less. Sea provinces are open water: only fleets — or land troops carried by fleets, or armies crossing via Part Sea — may enter them. A fleet can hold a sea province, so open water can be captured and owned like any other tile; only sea that no one has claimed belongs to no kingdom. The deep is not empty: sea provinces pay a modest tax (0–2), can be guarded by sea-beasts and their guardians, and hide their own features and treasure — a navy is worth building for more than defence.

Wealth

Each land province has a tax value — the gold it pays you each turn that you hold it. Provinces come in three broad bands:

BandTaxTypical neutral guard
Poor1–31–2 defenders
Prosperous4–62–3 defenders
Rich7–103–4 defenders

Your home province is the richest of all, at the top of the scale. The wealthier a province, the harder its neutral garrison fights for it.

Neutral guards

Most unclaimed provinces are held by a neutral guard — a small garrison, sometimes led by a guardian hero. To claim the province you must defeat them in battle with a force that includes armies (a lone hero can walk into an empty province, but cannot conquer a defended one). Beaten defenders don't always vanish at once, so budget more than one phase to secure a contested tile.

Exploration

A province has an exploration percentage for you, from 0% (never seen) to 100% (fully charted). Exploring reveals its terrain, wealth, defenders and — crucially — its hidden features, which surface gradually as your exploration climbs. Sending a hero to Explore, and the Explorer skill, drive this up.

↑ top

Features, Monsters & Treasure

Scattered across the map are features: monster lairs, ancient ruins, treasure sites and ritual grounds. They're the cheapest gold in the game and the only reliable source of permanent hero hit points and magic items. A hero who spends the early game clearing features becomes far more powerful than one who doesn't.

Searching a feature

Send a hero to Search Lair — investigate a discovered feature in their province. Many are guarded by a monster the hero must defeat in personal combat. The tougher the guardian, the richer the reward — gold, a bump to the hero's maximum HP, and a chance at a magic item. Guardians range from weak beasts a starting hero can handle to legendary monsters that will kill an unprepared champion.

The reward ladderWeaker guardians give modest gold, small permanent HP gains and a slim item chance. Stronger guardians pay handsomely and are far more likely to yield a magic item — including, at the very top, the world's Legendary relics. Features are found on both land and sea, so a strong navy opens the deep lairs to you as surely as a hero opens those ashore.

Every magic item in the game is unique — there is never more than one of a given item in the world at once, even on the largest maps. Once a relic has been found, no other lair will hold its twin; late in a long game, a picked-clean world may yield gold where a treasure once lay.

Ritual sites

Some features are ritual sites — standing stones of the old gods. Pagan kingdoms can draw on them through their Perform Rite action wherever one of their characters stands at a discovered site — the province need not be theirs. Other kingdoms can find ritual sites but cannot use them.

Part Three · Your People

Kings & Heroes

Your characters are your kings and heroes. Everywhere in these rules, "character" means both — anything said of heroes applies to your king too, unless stated otherwise.

The King

Your king is your most important character and, usually, your best. He leads armies, clears features, casts spells and performs your kingdom's king-only actions. He can do everything a hero can, and some things only he can. If your king falls, an heir is crowned in your wealthiest province at the start of the next turn — but an heir begins as a green, lightly-skilled ruler, nowhere near as mighty as the seasoned king he replaces, and you must name him, choose his temper and spend his skill points afresh. Your kingdom only truly ends if your king dies with no lands left to crown an heir in. Either way, weigh every risk you send your king into.

Heroes

Heroes are your working nobility: they command armies, scout, spy, steal, cast, and hunt monsters. Each has their own skills, temper, hit points and inventory. More heroes means more orders in play each turn, which is one of the deepest advantages in the game.

Hit points & recovery

Every character has current and maximum HP. They take damage in personal combat and heal by Resting, by winning fights (some tempers), and permanently raise their maximum by clearing features. A character reduced to zero HP may die — though some survive wounded, and the Cowardly may beg for mercy.

Hiring wandering heroes

Unaligned wandering heroes roam the map. Use the Hire Hero order when one of your characters shares their province to bring them into service for a per-turn wage. How much you pay — and whether you can hire them at all — depends on how far their alignment sits from yours: like-minded heroes are cheap, opposites are dear or refuse outright. (The Charm and Broker abilities open other routes to new heroes.)

Death & succession

When a hero dies their body lies where they fell — it can be looted, and (if not a king) even Resurrected by White magic. When a king dies, succession passes to an heir if one exists; if not, the kingdom is in mortal peril. Kings can never be resurrected or charmed.

The Fifteen Skills

Characters improve through fifteen skills in three groups. Each runs from level 0 to a cap of 9.

Martial

Melee · Archery · Tactical · March · Naval

Personal fighting, army command, and how far you move by land and sea.

Special

Spy · Thief · Druid · Sage · Explorer

Intelligence, theft, leading exotic troops, kingdom discoveries, and charting the map.

Magic

White · Psychic · Illusory · Elemental · Necromancy

The five schools of spellcraft. Your level in a school is how many, and which, spells you wield.

How skills work — the three caps

  • Levels 1–5 can be trained deliberately on the Practice ground (the Practice Skill order) — the reliable way to build a skill up on a schedule. Skills also rise on their own through field experience up to level 5, so a hero who fights, casts or explores often may reach 5 without ever setting foot on the Practice ground.
  • Levels 6–9 are won only by experience in the field — fighting, casting, exploring, leading. You cannot buy them; you must earn them.
  • The raw benefit of a skill caps at level 5. Levels 6–9 don't make the base effect bigger — instead each grants a distinct perk, and they count toward the Imperial Titles.

The mastery ladder (levels 6–9)

Every skill keeps rewarding you past 5, in the same shape: the even levels (6 and 8) grant quiet always-on perks, and the odd levels (7 and 9) grant a powerful new ability — a new order for most skills, or a new spell for the magic schools. What each one actually unlocks is deliberately left for you to discover in the field: only a hero who has pushed a skill to those heights learns what it bestows. They are earned by experience alone, never bought — and every level past 5 also counts toward the Imperial Titles.

Cross-training — the underrated habit

A hero is only as flexible as their skill spread. A pure warrior can't scout, spy or cast; a pure mage can't hold a battle line. Because levels 1–5 are cheap to Practice, spending a few early orders rounding out your characters — a little Tactical on a fighter, a little Explorer or Sage on everyone, a level of Druid so a leader can command mixed troops — pays off all game. See the first-three-turns advice.

Minor TitlesBeyond the eight Imperial Titles that win the game, there are twenty honorary Minor Titles — best duellist, best marksman, largest army, most magic items, and so on. Each is re-awarded every turn to the single best living character and grants a small personal perk. They don't win the game, but they make a specialist hero markedly better at what they do.
Spycraft

The Spy skill does far more than scout. A successful spy reveals the characters standing in the target province and their skills; spy a rival's home capital and you learn its income and may even steal one of its Sage discoveries for yourself (a higher Spy makes the theft more likely — the victim keeps theirs). Spying also decides aggressive encounters: catching a lone enemy hero is a contest of your Spy against theirs, not a certainty.

Spycraft cuts both ways. When you are spied, a counter-intelligence alert names the offending kingdom (though not the agent) — unless they moved in disguise. And a high-Spy character can slip through the enemy's passive watch unseen, vanishing from their sightings and maps — though a deliberate Spy on the right province will always find them.

Part Four · Giving Orders

Every Order Explained

Each character can take one action per phase. These are the orders you'll use most.

Movement & positioning

  • Move — travel to a single adjacent province. One Move order always advances you exactly one province, no matter how many movement points you have; points instead govern how many such steps you can make across the whole turn. Moving into a defended enemy or neutral province triggers a battle. Bringing armies lets you conquer.
  • Defend — stand ready to meet an assault for the rest of the turn. You hold your own province, and you also watch over its neighbours: if an enemy invades an adjacent province in your assigned zone, your general marches there to fight alongside its defenders (this costs a movement point, and a defeat ends his watch for the year).

Scouting & knowledge

  • Explore — chart the province you're in, raising your exploration of it and revealing its features.
  • Search Lair — investigate a discovered feature in your province, fighting its guardian for the reward. Lairs are found on land and at sea alike.
  • Spy — gather intelligence on a province: its defenders, owner, features and the characters there. Spy skill makes you better at it and harder to catch.

Armies & belongings

  • Recruit Armies — raise new units in a province you hold (limited per unit type, per province, per turn — see the wage rule).
  • Hire Hero — take a wandering hero in your province into service.
  • Disband Army — dismiss units you no longer wish to pay for.
  • Leave / Transfer Army — drop units as a garrison, or hand them to another of your characters in the same province.
  • Pick Up Army — take command of a garrison or another character's units where you stand.
  • Give Item — pass a magic item to another of your characters in the same province.

Confrontation

  • Encounter Character — name any enemy character you can see and hunt them down, chasing wherever they flee until you corner them. The meeting resolves as a personal duel — or, if both of you lead armies, as a full battle. A quarry still marching with a column won't break ranks to fight, and a king answers only a mutual challenge. (This order now does the work of the old Encounter and Pursue both.)
  • Steal — attempt to rob gold or an item from a character in your province (better with Thief; the Cowardly are natural thieves).

Power & self-improvement

  • Cast Spell — work one of your school's spells (see The Spellbook).
  • Use Item — activate a magic item's power.
  • Practice Skill — train a skill toward level 5 on the practice ground.
  • Rest — recover hit points.

Mastery — Levels 7 & 9

Push a non-magic skill to level 7, and again to level 9, and a powerful new order opens for that hero — a signature move that only a true master of the craft can perform. The five magic schools follow the same rhythm, gaining a new spell at each of those levels instead.

What each mastery order and spell actually does is a secret of the trade — you learn it when one of your heroes first reaches the level and the order appears on their sheet. Some are quiet edges; some reshape a battle; a few can only be turned against a kingdom you are openly at war with. Half the reward of raising a specialist to the heights is finding out what waits at the top.

No shortcutsLevels 7 and 9 cannot be trained on the Practice ground — they come only from hard field experience. A hero who has earned a mastery ability has genuinely lived through a great deal.

Part Five · War

Armies, Quality & the Wage Rule

Armies are made of units, each with a base strength and a quality tier. A character can lead a stack of units; units can also be left as a province garrison.

The kinds of unit

Every race raises its own named troops, but they fall into a handful of battlefield roles. Which you bring matters as much as how many — terrain, the pre-battle archery volley and sea crossings all turn on unit type.

RoleWhat it's for
Foot (light & heavy)The battle line. Light foot (e.g. Shieldwall Levies, Goblin Horde) are cheap and numerous; heavy foot (e.g. Dwarven Guard, Orc Berserkers) hit far harder for their cost. Steady on almost any ground.
CavalryShock troops (e.g. Free Lances, Gilded Lances, Warg Riders): powerful in open country, but they flounder in forest, jungle and mountain, where a battle line is squeezed and horses can't be brought to bear.
Archers & missileBowmen, crossbows and gun-crews (e.g. Yeoman Longbows, Elven Bowmen, Ironwood Crossbows, Mountain Guns). They fire a volley before the clash, thinning the enemy line, and are doubly telling against flyers — but they falter at sea.
FlyingWinged riders (e.g. Eagle Riders, Noble Griffons, Gnomish Gyrocopters, Wyvern Riders). They cross any terrain and even open sea without a fleet, and are hard for ground archers to bring down.
GarrisonCheap defensive militia. Strong for their price and the only unit you can raise two of per province per turn, but slow — the backbone of a stout defence and a cost-effective assault stack.
FleetsWarships (e.g. Galleon Fleet, Corsair Fleet, Dwarf Ironclads, Orcish Longships). The only units that fight in a sea province, and the only ones that can ferry land armies across water — one unit per hull.

Each race raises its own home roster. Beyond the mustered soldiery are the wild and the raised — powerful, but each with a catch:

CreaturesNoble wild beasts — great eagles, griffons, ents, giants, rocs, whale-cohorts. They need the Druid skill to lead (Druidic kingdoms command them freely), and a Druid can call them to the banner with Awaken the Wild.

Quality is a multiplier

A unit's strength is its base strength × its quality multiplier. Quality matters more than type.

TierMultiplier
Green×1
Average×2
Veteran×3
Crack×4
Elite×5

New recruits arrive Average. Units are promoted by winning battles and Tactical training, and demoted by losing. There is nothing below Green: a Green unit forced to demote disbands — the men simply go home, leaving no body and no grave to raise. (Skeletal units never change quality by any means.)

Race & the Druid requirement

A unit's race is the race of the province it was raised in. Any commander leads troops of their own race freely. Leading other races, and exotic Creatures, Monsters and fleets, needs the Druid skill — the more exotic the beast, the higher the level required. Fall short and that unit fights at a heavy penalty. Exemptions: skeletal units and neutral guards never suffer it, and Druidic kingdoms lead all Creatures freely.

The wage rule — the most important economic law in the game

Learn this or lose

Every unit costs its price to recruit, and the same price again every single turn it exists. Because tax is collected and wages paid in the same end-of-turn step, a unit raised this turn effectively costs double in its first season. Wages are owed on garrison units too, not just those in the field. (Skeletal units are the cheap exception, at 1 gold flat.)

If you can't pay your wages, your armies disband themselves, cheapest first, until the debt clears — and if that isn't enough, your heroes start to desert. This is the single most common way new players wreck their own kingdom.

How a Battle Is Fought

When forces meet, the battle resolves in a fixed sequence. You don't roll anything — you set your forces and orders, and the engine fights it out — but knowing the shape of it helps you plan.

  1. Pre-battle duel. Opposing commanders may face each other in single combat first, per the old traditions — a chance to wound or kill the enemy leader before a blow is struck.
  2. Battle magic. Eligible spells cast into the battle take effect — weakening enemy units, shielding your own, turning undead, and so on.
  3. Archery. Missile units loose before the lines close, thinning the enemy front.
  4. The strength clash. Each side's total effective strength is compared — base × quality, modified by Tactical command, Druid leadership, terrain, racial affinity and animosity, each race's combat gifts (elven woodland mastery in the trees, dwarven bulwark on defence), fanaticism, spells, temper and items. The front line's width is limited by terrain, so on tight ground (mountains) sheer numbers count for less.
  5. Margin & consequences. The winner's margin decides the toll: units on both sides are killed, promoted or demoted, and the loser may be forced to retreat. A decisive win can shatter an army; a narrow one is bloody for everyone.

Defenders fight with an edge on their own ground, and a home province defended by its owner is tougher still. Taking a contested province often takes more than one phase, because beaten defenders can stay and keep contesting it.

Personal Combat & Temper

When two characters fight — in a duel, an encounter, or a monster lair — it's resolved as personal combat: a series of rounds of archery and melee, shaped by each fighter's Melee and Archery skills, hit points, items, spells and, above all, their temper.

The four tempers

Temper governs how a character fights and when they run. All four are worth playing.

Berserk hits hardest and dies most. Brave is the complete soldier. Cautious is the hardest to kill. Cowardly is a poor swordsman but an excellent archer, thief and spy — and the safest temper for a king you cannot afford to lose.

Your encounter plan

Going into a personal fight you can set a plan — which combat spells to ready, and when to try to flee or to steal. A few things aren't yours to choose: every duel opens at spell range before the fighters close to melee, and a hero always carries all of his magic items into the fight — there is no leaving them behind. Even so, a well-judged plan wins fights a raw stat comparison would lose.

Duels can be lethalPersonal combat is not a bloodless contest of points — a character who is beaten badly enough can be slain outright, king or hero. This is why temper and hit points matter so much, why a Cowardly king is worth his caution, and why armour that turns a blow — a dwarf's mithril (−2 to every melee hit against the wearer), a healing item, a defensive spell — can be the difference between a wound and a grave.

Part Six · Power

Magic: The Five Schools

Magic is divided into five schools, each its own skill. Your level in a school determines which of its spells you can cast and how many spells you may cast per turn.

✨ White

Healing, blessing and holiness — resurrection, prosperity, and bane against the undead.

🧠 Psychic

The mind — charm, oratory, clairvoyance, and blasts that shatter skill and will.

🎭 Illusory

Deception — false terrain, phantom armies, hidden features, invisibility and mirror-selves.

🔥 Elemental

Destruction — lightning, fire, earthquake, flood, and summoned elementals.

💀 Necromancy

Death — commanding undead, raising skeletons from graves, curses and plague.

How spells are cast

Spells come in three flavours by when they work:

  • Action spells are cast as a phase order, targeting a character or province.
  • Combat spells are chosen in a personal-combat encounter plan and fire during a duel or lair fight.
  • Battle-magic spells are cast into a full army battle to swing it. Certain combat spells double as battle-magic, weakening whole enemy contingents.

The higher your school level, the more spells of that school you may cast per turn, and the true masters more still. Battlefield graves are left where armies have fallen; Necromancy draws its skeletons and control from these.

Magic Items & Discoveries

Magic items

Items are won from features, looted from the fallen, and stolen. They come in tiers, the rarest being the world's ten Legendary relics. A hero can carry several, and pass them to another character with Give Item. Broadly, items:

  • Boost a skill — adding levels of Melee, a magic school, Naval and so on (these levels even count toward the Titles).
  • Raise health, or heal.
  • Strengthen the armies a hero leads, or ease their movement.
  • Enrich your economy — extra tax, or gold per province.
  • Legendary specials — unique, powerful effects, and each relic held at the Reckoning is worth a victory point.

Every item is one of a kind: the world holds at most a single copy of each, so no two heroes will ever bear the same blade. A few relics are never found in lairs at all but granted — chief among them a dwarf kingdom's Mithril Armour, worn by every dwarven character as their birthright.

Sage discoveries

The Sage skill unlocks kingdom-wide discoveries — permanent upgrades that apply to your whole realm, in military, economy, espionage, scholarship and more (better recruit quality, richer provinces, stronger spies, and so on). They compound over the whole game, and the earliest ones you claim are the most valuable, so a little Sage from turn one pays for itself many times over. Rival spies can steal discoveries you haven't protected.

Part Seven · Gold

The Economy

Income

Your gold comes chiefly from the tax of the provinces you hold, plus bonuses from items, discoveries, titles and (for Neutral kingdoms) trade. Provinces conquered during a turn pay you at the end of that same turn.

Expenditure

You spend on recruiting new units, wages for every unit and hired hero you keep, hiring wandering heroes, and the odd purchased action (spying, raising graves). By far the biggest recurring cost is wages.

Order of operations — why it matters

At turn's end, tax and wages settle together. Because a newly recruited unit is paid its wage the same turn you raise it, the first season of any unit costs double. Plan your recruiting against the income you'll actually have after that double hit — including any tax from provinces you expect to take this turn.

BankruptcyIf you can't cover wages, units disband (cheapest first) and then heroes desert. Always keep your wage bill comfortably below your income, and disband deliberately rather than letting the shortfall choose for you.

Part Eight · Faith & Blood

The Seven Alignments

Alignment sets your battle fervour, who you can hire, and your signature action. Before each battle your force rolls for fanatical zeal; on a success it fights at ×1.30. Diplomacy & Alliances

Relations between kingdoms are one of three states: Neutral, Allied (Treaty), or at War. Each turn you may submit diplomacy declarations toward each rival, which resolve at end of turn.

  • Propose peace / alliance (Treaty). If both sides offer it, relations improve one step — from War to Neutral, or from Neutral to Allied.
  • Declare War. Any one declaration of war is enough — it takes two to make peace, but only one to break it.

Allies open their borders to one another and can pool forces, and — importantly — an allied bloc can jointly reach the dominance needed to Call the Reckoning. But allies do not share a victory: at the Reckoning every kingdom still competes for the throne. Expect late betrayals as the endgame nears. Certain aggressive mastery orders can only be used against a kingdom you are at war with.

↑ top

How to Win: The Reckoning

There is no turn limit. The game ends when one power comes to dominate the world and lays claim to the vacant Imperial throne — the Reckoning — or when only one kingdom remains.

Calling the Reckoning

A king-only action, unlocked once your kingdom — or the mutually-allied bloc you belong to — controls more than a dominance threshold of the world's total tax base. A single dominant kingdom may Call it alone; an allied bloc may Call it jointly.

What counts as the world's tax baseThe threshold is measured against the entire world's tax base — the tax of every province on the map, land and sea, both the ones players hold and the neutral provinces still out there to be conquered. Since sea provinces are now claimable and pay their own modest tax, the deep counts toward dominance just as the land does. Dominance therefore means truly commanding the world, not merely the slice that's been carved up so far — so early in a game, while much of the map is still neutral, the bar is genuinely hard to reach.

The threshold scales with the number of kingdoms — fewer players, higher bar:

The grace period — two turns to contest it

Calling the Reckoning doesn't end the game at once. It's announced to everyone, and the game is set to conclude at the end of the second full turn after the call. Those two turns are the endgame:

  • Rivals get a last chance to break the claim — if the claimant (or bloc) is driven back below threshold before it resolves, the claim lapses and play continues.
  • This is when trailing kingdoms ally against the leader, pool their armies, and try to shatter the dominant power — the classic "everyone turns on the front-runner" finish.
  • Those who can't stop it instead sprint to improve their own final standing.

The Reckoning is scored on Titles, not raw size

If dominance holds when the two turns are up, the world is judged. The winner is not simply the biggest empire — every kingdom is scored across eight Imperial Titles and the ten Legendary relics, and the greatest total Legacy wins the throne. Raw empire is the single biggest prize, but only one slice of the whole.

Part Nine · Strategy

Your First Three Turns

A strong opening is built on two facts. First, every unit costs double in its first season (raise cost, then a wage the same turn). Second, conquests are counted before tax is collected, so a province taken this turn pays you this turn. Together: recruit as much as your conquests will pay for, and no more.

Don't march out on turn 1 with your starting armyYour king's small starting force is not enough to take even a lightly-guarded province reliably. Attacking on a thin margin is a coin-flip at best; lose and you'll spend three turns recovering. Recruit first.

Turn 1 — recruit, explore, and blood a hero

  • Recruit at home first. Your rich home province lets you raise the most units. Garrison units are the best value in the game — cheap, strong for their price, and the only unit you can raise two of per province per turn. They're slow, but attacking an adjacent province costs only one movement point, which they can manage. Build a stack that turns a coin-flip assault into a near-sure thing — but stop short of spending everything, or the wage bill will bankrupt you and your new troops will disband themselves.
  • Search your home province's guaranteed feature. Send a hero to clear the weak guardian there: free gold, a permanent bump to the hero's max HP, and a shot at an item. This is the first step on your hero power curve — do it every turn there's a feature to clear.
  • Explore the province you mean to take next with another hero, so you can see its defenders before you commit.
  • Start cross-training. Spend a spare order to Practice a foundational skill — Sage (discoveries compound all game; the earliest are worth the most), a first level of Druid (so a leader can command mixed and captured troops), or Tactical on your main war-leader.
  • Attack in phase 3 or 4, not phase 5. Taking a defended province usually spans two phases even when you win every fight — start early enough to finish inside the turn.

Turn 2 — take the poor province first, then compound

  • Target the poor province, not the rich one. Poor provinces have the fewest defenders; your odds collapse far faster than the tax reward rises as guards get thicker. A poor tile taken safely beats a rich tile you fail to take. Save prosperous provinces for turn 3, once your stacks are bigger.
  • Concentrate — don't split. One strong stack taking provinces in sequence beats two weak stacks attacking in parallel. Two 50% attacks are worse than one 90% attack followed by another.
  • Now you hold two provinces, so your recruiting capacity has doubled. Raise garrisons in both, still keeping wages well under income.
  • Keep clearing features. Your feature-hunting hero should be on their second or third lair by now — noticeably tougher, and probably carrying an item.

Turn 3 — the shape of your game emerges

  • Garrison what you take. A conquered province left empty is retaken for free by a single enemy hero. Leaving a cheap garrison unit behind is the cheapest insurance in the game.
  • Now Tactical earns its keep. Once your war-stacks reach five or six units, Tactical is the best gold-free multiplier there is — it costs orders, not coin. Keep training it on your main leader.
  • Keep cross-training the rest. A pinch of Explorer on scouts (faster charting, auto-spotting features), Spy on an intelligence hero, a magic school on a caster. Rounded heroes give you more good options every single turn, and every skill you push past 5 later feeds an Imperial Title.
  • Pick a direction. Look at where the neutral wild country and the other players are. Expanding toward a neighbour invites war before you're ready; expanding into the wild buys time, though primal guardians are tougher.
  • Watch the wage line every turn. If wages ever creep above income, disband something on purpose before the engine disbands it for you.

Ten things new players get wrong

  1. Marching out on turn 1 with the starting army — it usually fails.
  2. Spending all their gold on turn 1 — the wage bill lands the same turn and the new troops disband.
  3. Forgetting that garrisons cost wages too, not just field armies.
  4. Splitting forces — two half-strength attacks lose to one strong attack repeated.
  5. Attacking mountains with a huge stack — the battle line is narrow, so numbers can't all fight.
  6. Marching cavalry into forest, where they fight badly.
  7. Expecting to take a province in a single phase — budget two.
  8. Leaving a conquered province empty — it's taken back for free.
  9. Ignoring features — the cheapest gold, and the only source of permanent hero HP and items.
  10. Never practising Sage — dozens of permanent kingdom-wide upgrades, and the compounding starts the turn you begin.
The habit that winsEvery turn, clear a feature, take a province you can take safely, keep wages under income, and spend one spare order improving a skill. Do that consistently and you'll be the dominant power when it's time to Call the Reckoning.

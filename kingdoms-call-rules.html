Part One · The Basics

The Game at a Glance

Kingdoms Call is a slow-burn fantasy grand-strategy game played by email. Each of you rules a realm on a shared, hidden map. Every round you write a set of orders for your king and heroes; when every player has sent theirs, the world resolves them all at once and returns a private chronicle of what your people saw and did. Then you write the next round.

You expand by exploring the fog-shrouded map, conquering the neutral provinces that fill it, and clearing the monster lairs and ruins hidden in the wild. You grow stronger by recruiting armies, training your heroes, and hoarding gold — while watching the rival realms doing exactly the same.

There is no fixed number of turns. The game ends at the Reckoning: when one realm (or an allied bloc) comes to dominate the world, its king may lay claim to the vacant Imperial throne. The claimant is then judged against every rival across thirteen Imperial Titles and the Legendary relics, and the realm with the greatest legacy is crowned. Full detail below.

How a Turn Works

A turn represents a season. Within it, time passes in five phases. You give each character a list of up to five orders — one per phase — and they carry them out in order, phase 1 through phase 5.

Inside a phase

Every character in the world acts in the same phase before the next phase begins. Movement, battles, spells, recruiting and everything else for phase 1 resolve, then phase 2, and so on. This is why when you do something matters as much as what you do: a hero who moves in phase 1 can be somewhere useful by phase 3; a spell cast in phase 2 can shape a battle in phase 4.

Order matters — dependency and initiative

Within a phase, everything happens in a fixed sequence. You don't manage it yourself, but knowing it lets you plan a phase with confidence:

  1. Unfinished battles resume. Any province still contested from the previous phase is fought over again before anyone new acts.
  2. Invasions are announced and defenders react. A general whose province is about to be invaded by a faster enemy is pinned in place to fight (unless on Defend). Generals on Defend take note of every incursion into their watch (they ride out a little later — see the next step), and characters ordered to reinforce a threatened province arrive early, so they are standing there before the invader marches in.
  3. Orders resolve, in three tiers. First every order that does not relocate a character — casting, recruiting, practising, researching, searching lairs, spying, giving and using items, hiring, disbanding and the like — settles while everyone still stands where the phase began. Then every Move and Encounter Character resolves, and the battles they start are fought on arrival. Characters on Defend are counted last. So a co-located caster always gets to act before a fast general marches out of the province. Between the two sits the Defend step: once every non-moving order has resolved — including any spell, column or item aimed at a general on Defend, which therefore finds him still at home — the generals on Defend ride out to the invasions announced at the top of the phase, and any host an enemy raised, summoned or seized inside a watched province during the order step now has to fight the general who has arrived to defend it. Only then do the Moves resolve. The general is always in the line before an invader arrives and before a raised host can take the ground (see The Defend order in full).
  4. Within a tier, initiative decides. Characters act from highest initiative to lowest: initiative is the sum of all a character's skill levels, with March counting three times. Ties go to the older character (the lower character number). A hero with the higher initiative recruits, casts or marches before a slower one — which matters when two forces race for the same province, or two casters target the same hero.
  5. Dependencies override the tiers. A few pairings are ordered by need rather than speed: a character handing an item or troops to a co-located ally acts before that ally; a character marching in to receive something arrives before the giver gives; a Pick Up waits for any Leave in the same province; and a pursuer (Encounter Character) acts immediately after the character they are chasing, wherever they went.
  6. After all orders. As a last sweep, a general on Defend who has not yet reacted this phase gives battle to any enemy host that has ended up inside his watch by some road the earlier steps did not catch; lone characters on Defend spring their ambushes; then the phase's incidental skill-rises are reported and the next phase begins.
  7. War fronts — end of phase 1 only. Any army that began the turn on the ground of a realm it is at war with, or in the same province as an army of such a realm, and is still standing there once every phase-1 order has resolved, now gives battle for that province — see War fronts.

The practical upshot: a plan spread sensibly across the phases beats one crammed into a single phase, and where two characters must interact in the same phase, put the giver's action in the same phase as the receiver's and the engine will sequence them.

End of turn

After phase 5, the season closes: tax is collected and wages are paid (in that combined step), diplomacy declarations take effect, titles are re-awarded, and temporary effects expire. Then your chronicle is written and the next turn opens.

When the turn is resolved

The GM chooses how each game's turns are run. In a manual game the GM resolves the turn by hand once orders are in. In an automatic game the turn resolves on its own as soon as every realm still in contention has sent its orders. In an automatic game with a deadline, the turn also resolves at a fixed clock time whether or not every realm has sent orders — your portal shows the next deadline in your own local time. Characters whose orders are not in when the turn resolves simply hold (Defend) for the season — and that is a real Defend order: any phase you leave blank is filled with Defend, so an idle general keeps a watch and will ride out to a threatened neighbour exactly as described under The Defend order in full. Realms that have conceded or whose dynasty has ended are never waited on.

RememberProvinces you conquer during a turn are counted before that turn's tax is collected — so a province taken in phase 3 pays you at the end of the very same turn. Conquest can fund itself within the turn.

Founding Your Realm

Before the game begins you shape your realm with four choices.

Race

Human, Elven, Dwarven or Orcish. Your race sets the flavour of your home troops, the units you'll raise cheaply at home, and a distinct set of racial gifts — from the woodland-fighting of the elves to the mithril of the dwarves. It is not a straitjacket: you can lead other races with the Druid skill, and troops you recruit take the race of the province they're raised in, not yours. Race is important enough to have its own chapter — see The Four Races.

Alignment

One of seven moral stances — Divine, Good, Druidic, Neutral, Pagan, Evil, Undead — running from holy to unholy. Alignment decides your fanaticism in battle, which heroes you can afford to hire, and your realm's unique signature action. This is the single most flavour-defining choice; see The Seven Alignments.

Your king's temper

Berserk, Brave, Cautious or Cowardly — how your king behaves in personal combat. Every temper is viable; see Personal Combat & Temper. A Cowardly king is a poor duellist but the safest to keep alive, which matters, because losing your king is catastrophic.

Your king's skills

Your king has 4 skill points to assign across the fifteen skills (your alignment nudges one of them toward its signature lane). This is your opening strategy in miniature: points in Sage start the long game of realm-wide discoveries; points in Tactical or Druid make your armies hit harder; points in a magic school put spells in your hand from turn one.

What you start with

Every realm opens from the same footing:

  • A home province — the richest tile you'll be handed, fully explored and yours, with a starting income of 10 gold per turn. It recruits every land unit of your race, whatever its terrain (other provinces offer only a couple of local types); a fleet is on offer there only if the home is coastal.
  • A starting treasury of 15 gold.
  • Your king (with 4 skill points to assign, as above) and a small starting army under their command.
  • Two heroes, each of whom begins with 3 skill points spread at random across the skills — a pair of ready lieutenants to lead armies, explore, cast or train.

Everything else on the map you must go and take.

Part One · Blood & Kin

The Four Races

Four peoples contest the vacant throne, and each fights the world in its own way. Where alignment is the soul of your realm, race is its blood — it colours the troops you raise at home, the ground you were born to, and a handful of gifts no other people share. Two of those gifts belong to every race alike — the pull of kinship and the weight of old grudges — and the rest are each people's own.

Kinship & the banner (all races)

Soldiers fight harder among their own kind and under a lord of their own blood. Two separate bonuses reward keeping your kin together, and they stack:

A king personally leading troops of their own race therefore fields them at ×1.44 — a powerful reason to keep your monarch at the head of a home-blood army. Because recruits take the race of the province they're raised in, a conquered foreign province gives you foreign troops: strong soldiers, but strangers to your crown who earn no King's-kin bonus until you lead them with one of their own.

Ancient grudges

Two feuds are older than any realm. Elves and Dwarves will not serve one another gladly, and neither will Humans and Orcs. An army led by a commander of its rival race fights at −20%. Leading captured elven bowmen with a dwarven hero is possible — but they will resent every order.

The peoples

Each race is born to a signature terrain (where most of its provinces are found) and carries two gifts of its own, which apply to that people serving a realm of their own race.

🏰Human — the Adaptable

Home ground: plains. Humans hold no single mastery but learn faster than anyone — the race that rises by effort rather than birthright. Their armies field balanced foot, cavalry and the versatile Free Lances.

  • Drillmasters — human units improve their experience level twice as readily, both from a commander's drills and from surviving battle.
  • Quick learners — human characters gain skill levels 50% faster, in every skill and by every means.

🌲Elven — the Woodland Folk

Home ground: forest. Elves are archers and scouts without peer, deadliest among the trees and swift to chart the wild places. Their rosters lean on bowmen, wardens and eagle-riders.

  • Woodland mastery — an elven realm's own troops fight at +50% in forest and jungle, their home ground.
  • Homeward Flight — an elven realm's elven unit that would be killed in battle may instead slip away and make for home. The chance depends on the terrain fought over: 30% in desert or mountain, 45% in plains, 60% in forest or jungle. Survivors are gathered at the very end of the turn and delivered to the king's home province (or, if that has been lost, the realm's wealthiest remaining province), rejoining its garrison — so long as the realm still holds any land at all.

⛰️Dwarven — the Mountain Kin

Home ground: mountain. Dwarves are the great defenders and finest smiths, unbreakable behind a shield wall and clad in armour no other race can forge. Their line is heavy foot, crossbows and the mighty Mountain Guns.

  • Bulwark — when defending, dwarven armies fight at ×1.5, on top of the usual defender's edge — a dug-in dwarf hold is a nightmare to storm.
  • Mithril Armour — every dwarven character bears a suit of mithril (granted at the game's start, and to any dwarf hired into the realm). Melee blows against the wearer do 2 less damage.

🔥Orcish — the War Horde

Home ground: desert. Orcs live for the attack and grow stronger the longer the war runs. They swarm with berserkers, warg-riders, goblin hordes and wyverns, and pay for their wars with plunder.

  • War-spoils — orcish units that win a battle as part of an attacking force plunder the field: they cost only half wages that turn.
  • Iron constitution — orcish characters serving an orcish realm have 20% more maximum HP than another character of the same skills (see Hit points), and the usual limit of 100 stretches to 120 for them.
Choosing a raceRace and alignment are chosen separately, so any pairing is legal — but some sing. Dwarven + Good makes an immovable defensive wall; Orcish + Evil is relentless aggression that pays for itself; Elven + Druidic rules the deep woods; Human + anything leans on the fastest heroes and troops in the game. Play to your blood: keep your kin under a lord of their own race, and fight on the ground you were born to.

Part Two · The World

The World & Provinces

The map is a grid of provinces, most of it hidden under fog of war at the start. You see only what your characters have visited or scouted; everything else is guesswork until you send someone to look. Intelligence you gather (and steal) persists across turns.

The size of the world

The map is a wrapped hex grid, kept as small as the placement rules allow so realms meet early and no campaign is spent marching through empty country. Its size follows the number of realms: 2 realms 8×5 (40 provinces), 3 → 8×7 (56), 4 → 9×7 (63), 5 → 12×7 (84), 6 → 15×7 (105), 7 → 16×7 (112), 8 → 17×8 (136), 9 and 10 → 16×11 (176). Each of these is the smallest grid on which every capital can still be set at least three neutral provinces away from every other, so no two kings ever open on each other’s doorstep. Roughly 40% of it is sea, laid out as coherent coasts and inland seas; the rest is land shared exactly equally between the four humanoid races, with a portion of wild Primal (Creature / Monster) country. Every home province starts adjacent to one poor and one prosperous humanoid province, at least one of them the king's own race, plus a sea province for a king with any Naval skill. At most one Primal province ever borders a home (the Pagan or Druidic starting neighbour, where one is placed). No home ever borders more than two sea provinces, and a coastal home always has at least one open, undefended sea beside it, so a first fleet has somewhere to sail without a fight.

Terrain

Provinces are plains, forest, jungle, mountain, desert or sea. Terrain shapes which troops thrive there and which suffer — cavalry flounder in forest, missile troops falter at sea, and mountains squeeze a battle line so numbers count for less. Sea provinces are open water: only fleets — or land troops carried by fleets, or armies crossing via Part Sea — may enter them. A fleet can hold a sea province, so open water can be captured and owned like any other tile; only sea that no one has claimed belongs to no realm. The deep is not empty: sea provinces pay a modest tax (0–2), can be guarded by sea-beasts and their guardians, and hide their own lairs and treasure — a navy is worth building for more than defence, and every sea battle your admiral wins counts toward The Sea Lord.

Wealth

Each land province has a tax value — the gold it pays you each turn that you hold it. Provinces come in three broad bands:

Your home province is the richest of all, at the top of the scale. The wealthier a province, the harder its neutral garrison fights for it.

Neutral guards

Most unclaimed provinces are held by a neutral guard — a small garrison, sometimes led by a guardian hero, the local chieftain. To claim the province you must defeat them in battle with a force that includes armies (a lone hero can walk into an empty province, but cannot conquer a defended one). Beaten defenders don't always vanish at once, so budget more than one phase to secure a contested tile. Breaking a chieftain's guard is a real battle in every sense: it earns your commander Grand Marshal merit ashore and Sea Lord merit at sea, exactly as a war with a rival realm would.

Exploration

A province has an exploration percentage for you, from 0% (never seen) to 100% (fully charted). Exploring reveals its terrain, wealth, defenders and — crucially — its hidden lairs, which surface gradually as your exploration climbs. Sending a hero to Explore, and the Explorer skill, drive this up.

Lairs, Monsters & Treasure

Scattered across the map are lairs: monster lairs, ancient ruins, treasure sites and ritual grounds. Clearing a lair is the main source of permanent hero maximum-HP gains and magic items, alongside gold. Lairs are revealed by exploration and appear on both land and sea.

Searching a lair

Send a hero to Search Lair — investigate a discovered lair in their province. Many are guarded by a monster the hero must defeat in personal combat. The tougher the guardian, the richer the reward — gold, a roll toward hardening the hero (see Hit points), field experience in whichever of Melee or Archery they fought with, and a magic item. Every lair's hoard is fixed when the world is made: its gold and whatever magic lies there are decided from the start, so a hero who goes Hunting Rumours in the province can learn exactly what defeating the guardian will yield — and whether a thief has already been through. A Steal tactic lifts a share of the gold and, with luck (30% + 10% per Thief level), one of the hoard's items — never a Legendary relic, which only the guardian's defeat will yield. Beware: guardians of humanoid kind — bandits, orcs, giants, fiends, the intelligent undead — turn the hoard's magic against you, wielding its blades and drinking its potions; fey and dragons wear only its trinkets; beasts and vermin ignore it. Guardians take exactly one action a round, like you. Guardians range from weak beasts a starting hero can handle to legendary monsters that will kill an unprepared champion.

Know what you are fightingEvery lair in your Known Lairs card, in the map popup and in the Search Lair dropdown names its guardian and the kind of creature it is — undead, fiend, giant, dragon, beast, vermin, fey, construct, elemental, aberration or humanoid. That word is worth reading before you commit a hero. It tells you whether your White magic will bite double (undead and fiends), whether the thing can pick up the hoard's own sword and use it on you (humanoids, the undead, giants and fiends can; fey and dragons wear only its trinkets; beasts, vermin, constructs, elementals and aberrations use nothing at all), and whether a Druid's reading of a natural animal will help you (it works on beasts and vermin, and not on the undead, demons, constructs, dragons, giants or fey).

How strong a hero does a lair need?

The four grades are cut to four heroes. Send a hero of the grade's own measure and the hardest guardian of that grade falls to them about 19 fights in 20; the gentler guardians of the grade more often still. The four benchmarks below are the whole of the answer — meet them and a lair of that grade is a calculated risk rather than a gamble.

Reading the table

  • Hit points are the first thing to check, and the slowest to buy. They come only from winning fights (see Hit points), and the climb stalls around 60. A hero at the right skill level but half the hit points is not ready — they will win the exchanges and still bleed out. If one figure is short, let it be the skill, never the HP.
  • The kit is one grade BELOW the lair. A Legendary guardian is measured against a hero in Powerful-grade gear, not against one already carrying relics — the Legendary relics are the reward for clearing a Legendary lair, not the price of entry. The same holds all the way down: Weak items are what you take into a Moderate lair.
  • "A school at N" means any one of the five, and the weakest of them. White, Psychic, Illusory, Elemental and Necromancy sit within about a point of each other across the whole bestiary, so pick the school you like; the benchmark holds for all five. What the school buys in a lair fight is a reliable extra action — and against a guardian weak to it, a cast that cannot fail and does double everything.
  • A hero with no magic at all can still clear every grade — but they are not the hero the grade was cut against, and they need more of everything else: more hit points, better kit, and a level or two more Melee. A pure swordsman is a slower, steadier climb.
  • Archery is whatever the mix leaves you. The benchmarks are written around Melee because that is what a lair fight ends with, but a bow is a real weapon here: from Archery 3 you loose two arrows an action, every level adds +5% to holding the enemy at range, and a foe who closes on you eats a free arrow first. An archer-first hero clears the same grades on the same hit points.
  • Temper is a fifth statistic. Every figure above is a Brave hero's, and a Berserk one matches it. Cautious and Cowardly heroes break off below 40% health, and a hero who runs leaves the guardian standing on its hoard — which counts as a failure however healthy they walk home. If you send a cautious hero, send them a grade under their measure.

If you are short of the mark

The odds do not fall gently. A hero one benchmark short of the grade is not at nine fights in ten; they are somewhere well below it, and a Legendary guardian will simply kill them. Three ways to close the gap, in the order they are worth doing:

  1. Clear more lairs of the grade below. This is the engine of the whole climb — it is the only thing that raises maximum HP, it pays the items for the next grade, and the fighting itself raises Melee or Archery. Reckon on about eight Weak lairs to be ready for Moderate, a dozen Moderate lairs to be ready for Powerful, and half a dozen Powerful ones to be ready for Legendary.
  2. Practise between lairs. Melee 9 with a school at 7 is more skill than kills alone will hand you, so the upper rungs genuinely expect a hero to spend some orders on the practice ground. Levels 1–5 can be trained; 6–9 come only from the field, so train the school early and let the fighting carry the blade.
  3. Learn the guardian's weakness first. Spend a phase Hunting Rumours in the province, or send a hero of the right blood or creed. A guardian fought on its weakness is very roughly half the fight — see below.
No guardian fells a hero with one blowHowever monstrous the creature, no single attack by a lair guardian may take more than a third of your character's maximum hit points. A fresh 15 HP hero loses at most 5 to any one blow, a 30 HP hero at most 10, a 60 HP veteran at most 20. You can still be worn down and killed over a long fight — but you can no longer be deleted by one roll from a Legendary beast, so a high-tier lair is a calculated risk rather than a coin-flip. The reduced figure is simply the damage your report shows; there is no separate note about it. This limit applies only to a guardian's blows against a character — never to your own blows, and never in a duel between two characters.

Every guardian has a weakness. One exploitable flaw, fixed for that kind of creature everywhere in the world, and never a weakness to plain melee — blades are what everyone swings anyway. A weakness is one of:

  • A school of magic. Spells of that school always take hold against it — the cast never fails — and their damage and their effects both double: twice the burn, twice the to-hit penalty, twice the rounds of stun or silence. All undead guardians are open to White Magic, and so are many fiends; the rest are spread across Necromancy, Psychic, Illusory and Elemental according to their nature. Constructs shatter under elemental force, the fey wither under death-magic, dull-witted giants fall for illusions, and a dragon's vast and prideful mind lies open to Psychic magic.
  • Archery. Arrows strike it for double damage.
  • A race. A character of that blood — Human, Elven, Dwarven or Orcish — deals double damage to it with everything.
  • An alignment. A character serving a realm of that alignment deals double damage to it with everything.

Learning a weakness. You are not told it up front. Two things reveal it, and either is permanent: go Hunting Rumours in the province and the tavern-talk may name it, or simply use the right attack and watch it bite — the first time a weakness tells, your hero sees why. Once learned, the lesson is your realm's for the rest of the game and is keyed to the kind of creature, not the lair: learn what kills one Barrow Wight and every Barrow Wight in the world is marked on your Known Lairs list thereafter. Send the wrong hero and a Legendary guardian is a death sentence; send the one it cannot stand and the same fight is half the length.

Hunting Rumours — asking before you fight

You need not walk into a lair blind. A character standing in a province with known lairs may spend a phase Hunting Rumours — a season in the taverns and on the roads, asking after the things that guard those lairs. Tick as many of the province's known lairs as you like on the order; each costs gold by its grade — Weak 1g, Moderate 2g, Powerful 3g, Legendary 4g — paid up front and in the order listed, until the purse runs dry. For every lair paid for, your character tries to learn three things, each rolled separately at 50% + 10% per level of Spy (Spy counted to 5, so never above the 95% ceiling):

  1. The guardian's weakness — and, once heard, your realm marks every creature of that kind with it for the rest of the game, exactly as if you had seen it bite.
  2. The hoard — the exact gold defeating the guardian will pay, what magic lies in the lair (and whether a thief has already lifted it), and which of those items the guardian will turn against you.
  3. Your own chances — how this character, as they stand today, would fare against the guardian. This is not a guess: the world fights the lair many times over in the background and reports the result as one of five bands, from no real chance, through the odds are against you, it could go either way and the odds favour you, to almost certain. Change the hero's kit, hit points or skills and the answer changes with them.

A failed roll is simply a fact not learned — the gold is spent either way. A season of asking questions is Spy fieldwork, and may raise that skill as any use of it does.

The reward ladderWeaker guardians give modest gold (5–8); stronger ones pay better (Moderate 12–18, Powerful 20–30, Legendary 40–60). Every lair holds exactly one magic item — always one, never two, never none — and it is of the guardian's own grade. Items come in four tiers named for the guardians that keep them: Weak, Moderate, Powerful and Legendary. A Weak lair holds a Weak item; beat a Legendary guardian and you take a Legendary relic, one of the named treasures of the world — and each relic you still hold at a Reckoning is worth a point. There are always twenty-two Legendary lairs in a world, whatever its size, and so always the full twenty-two relics — each one a thing no other item in the game can do, and worth about twice a Powerful item of its kind. Those lairs are set as far apart as the map allows, so no one realm begins within reach of them all. Difficulty is spread by distance from the nearest capital, but it is a mix at every distance rather than clean rings of one grade. In and immediately around the home provinces the country runs about 60% Weak, 30% Moderate and 10% Powerful — so a new king has somewhere safe to blood a hero, and the occasional real monster on his own doorstep. The further out you go the fewer Weak lairs and the more Moderate and Powerful ones, until the deep wilds are almost all dangerous. The world's totals are unchanged by this arrangement: the map holds the same proportion of each grade however it is laid out. At every other tier the treasury is dealt so that weapons, armour, shields, helms, cloaks, rings, amulets, combat items and unslotted trinkets are found in equal measure. Every slot offers the same number of items — weapons, armour, shields, helms, cloaks, rings, amulets and combat items alike — and there are enough of them to stock even the largest map without a kind running short. The dealing is done region by region, each realm's own neighbourhood of lairs getting an even spread of every kind, so no realm spends the game turning up nothing but rings; and a find made in play is drawn from whichever kind that realm has seen least of. Lairs are found on both land and sea, so a strong navy opens the deep lairs to you as surely as a hero opens those ashore.

Every magic item in the game is unique — there is never more than one of a given item in the world at once, even on the largest maps. Once a relic has been found, no other lair will hold its twin; late in a long game, a picked-clean world may yield gold where a treasure once lay.

Ritual sites

Some lairs are ritual sites — standing stones of the old gods. Pagan realms can draw on them through their Perform Rite action wherever one of their characters stands at a discovered site — the province need not be theirs. Other realms can find ritual sites but cannot use them. Every primal (Creature or Monster) land province holds a ritual site, where both rites can be performed; about a quarter of humanoid land provinces hold one too, but with no wild creatures there to bind only the Blood Offering is possible at those stones.

One rite per province per turn. Once a rite has been performed in a province, the stones there fall silent until the turn is over: any later rite in that province the same turn fails, whichever realm or character attempts it (including your own). Only a rite that actually takes place counts — one that fails for want of gold, creatures or units leaves the stones free. Rites resolve in phase order, so the earliest phase wins; within the same phase, the character with the higher initiative acts first.

Sight, Fog & Intelligence

The map is hidden. You see a province, and what stands in it, only through the sources below. Intelligence you gather persists across turns as remembered (possibly out-of-date) information; live information is only shown where you can see the province at the end of the turn.

Which provinces you can see

  • Owned — provinces you hold. Full current information.
  • Occupied — a province where one of your characters stands at the end of the turn. Full current information.
  • Bordering — a province adjacent to land you own or occupy. Current terrain, ownership and wealth; no enemy units or characters shown.
  • Remembered — a province one of your characters has seen before but cannot see now. Shown frozen at its last-seen state; no live units or characters.
  • Spied — a province you ran a Spy action on this turn. Reveals its defenders, owner, lairs and the characters present, including unit types and qualities.

Certain Sage discoveries can widen this passive sight further.

Seeing other players' characters

Whether an enemy character standing in a province is shown to you depends on how you see that province:

  • You have a character in the province (you are co-located): the enemy is always seen.
  • You spied the province this turn: the enemy is always seen (a deliberate spy pierces concealment).
  • The enemy is leading an army in a province you own: always seen — an army in your land means a battle is being fought there.
  • An enemy character is lurking in a province you own but have no character in: your garrison and subjects may or may not spot them — this is a Spy contest, resolved by the formula below.
  • Allied and neutral-realm characters standing in a province you own are always seen.
  • Wandering heroes and neutral guards (belonging to no realm) follow the older concealment rule: a high-Spy character may pass through unseen unless you spy the province.

Spying and counter-intelligence

A Spy action reveals the characters in the target province and their skills. Spying a rival's home capital also reveals its income and can steal one of its Sage discoveries (the victim keeps theirs; the theft lands at 10% + 10% per level of Spy, clamped 5–95%). When you are spied, a counter-intelligence alert names the offending realm (not the individual agent) — unless the spy moved in disguise. A high-Spy character may slip through an enemy's passive watch unseen, but a deliberate Spy on the right province still finds them.

Part Three · Your People

Kings & Heroes

Your characters are your kings and heroes. Everywhere in these rules, "character" means both — anything said of heroes applies to your king too, unless stated otherwise.

The King

Your king is your most important character and, usually, your best. They lead armies, clear lairs, cast spells and perform your realm's king-only actions. They can do everything a hero can, and some things only they can. If your king falls, an heir is crowned in your wealthiest province at the start of the next turn — but an heir begins as a green, lightly-skilled ruler, nowhere near as mighty as the seasoned king they replace, and you must name them, choose their temper and spend their skill points afresh. Your realm only truly ends if your king dies with no lands left to crown an heir in. Either way, weigh every risk you send your king into.

Heroes

Heroes are your working nobility: they command armies, scout, spy, steal, cast, and hunt monsters. Each has their own skills, temper, hit points and inventory. More heroes means more orders in play each turn, which is one of the deepest advantages in the game.

Hit points & recovery

Every character has current and maximum HP. They take damage in personal combat and do not recover on their own — wounds heal by Resting, by certain discoveries, items and spells, and (for the Brave) a once-per-fight second wind. Winning a fight does not heal you. A character reduced to zero HP may die — though some survive wounded, and the Cowardly may beg for mercy.

Maximum HP is EARNED, not trained. A character's maximum hit points do not follow their skills at all — they are won by fighting. Every character starts at 15 (18 for an Orcish character of an Orcish realm) and rises only through combat victories: clearing a lair guardian, slaying an enemy character, or holding the field in a pre-battle duel. Each such win rolls for a gain, and the gains get harder as you toughen:

The practical shape of it: a fresh hero at 15 HP toughens quickly and reliably, a veteran in the forties still gains most fights, and past sixty the climb is slow and uncertain. A hero who never fights never gets tougher, however many skills they train — which is the whole point. Levels buy what a character can do; only blood buys what they can take.

Fighting pays twice over: every won fight is field experience in whichever of Melee or Archery you fought with (the ordinary chance to rise a level), as well as the hardening roll above. Mastery also tells in the blow itself: a character strikes harder for every level of Melee (or Archery) they hold, so seasoned fighters end fights sooner rather than trading scratches with a tough opponent.

Hiring wandering heroes

Unaligned wandering heroes roam the map. Use the Hire Hero order when one of your characters shares their province to bring them into service for a per-turn wage. How much you pay — and whether you can hire them at all — depends on how far their alignment sits from yours: like-minded heroes are cheap, opposites are dear or refuse outright. (The Charm and Broker abilities open other routes to new heroes.)

Wandering heroes grow while they waitA hero nobody has hired does not stand still. From the end of turn 5 onward, every neutral hero — the wanderers and the local chieftains alike — has a 50% chance each turn of gaining one skill point, and never more than one. A hero sharing a province with another neutral hero learns from them: the skill raised is the other hero's highest (one of them at random if several tie). A hero alone hones one of the skills they already have, chosen at random. So the wanderer you passed over on turn 3 may be a far better prospect — and a far dearer wage — by turn 12, and two chieftains who share a border will slowly come to share each other's crafts.

Death & succession

When a hero dies their body lies where they fell — it can be looted, and (if not a king) even Resurrected by White magic. When a king dies, succession passes to an heir if one exists; if not, the realm is in mortal peril. Kings can never be charmed. The one exception to succession: a Divine realm's king is not replaced while the realm holds consecrated ground — the divine return them there (see Divine, below).

The crowning of an heir is your realm's business alone. The world learns that a king has fallen, but the summons to name your heir, choose their temper and spend their remaining skill points goes only to you.

How skills work — the three caps

  • Levels 1–5 can be trained deliberately on the Practice ground (the Practice Skill order) — the reliable way to build a skill up on a schedule. Skills also rise on their own through field experience up to level 5, so a hero who fights, casts or explores often may reach 5 without ever setting foot on the Practice ground.
  • Levels 6–9 are won only by experience in the field — fighting, casting, exploring, leading. You cannot buy them; you must earn them. A skill can rise through experience at most once per phase, and at most once across a battle and the carry-on battles that follow it; and each time a skill has already risen this way in a turn, the next chance to raise it the same way is ×0.8 (cumulative until the turn ends).
  • The raw benefit of a skill caps at level 5. Levels 6–9 don't make the base effect bigger — instead each grants a distinct perk, and they count toward the Imperial Titles.

Four things ignore that benefit cap, and they are the reason mastery is worth chasing: a magic school's chance to cast keeps climbing +10% a level to the 95% ceiling; Melee and Archery to-hit keep climbing at +5% a level past 5; Melee and Archery damage read your raw level, so a Melee 9 hero hits harder than a Melee 5 one; and the Imperial Titles count skill levels all the way to 9. Those same seven skills — Melee, Archery and the five schools — are also the only ones an item can carry past 5.

What a magic item can add on top

Because trained benefit stops at 5, an item that grants skill levels normally stops there too: a Tactical 5 general who finds a +2 tome still commands at Tactical 5, and the same is true of Naval, Druid, Sage, Spy, Thief, Explorer and March.

Seven skills are the exception — Melee, Archery and the five magic schools. For those, item and aura bonuses stack on top of your trained 5, all the way to the ceiling of 9, because levels past 5 genuinely do something there. A Melee 4 hero wielding a Legendary +4 blade under a Crown of Command fights at an effective Melee 9.

For the magic schools what that buys is cast chance and nothing else — +10% a level to the 95% ceiling. A relic focus makes a master reliable; it does not make their Fireball burn hotter.

The mastery ladder (levels 6–9)

Every skill keeps rewarding you past 5 — always-on perks at the even levels, a new order or spell at the odd ones — but nothing here says what they do: each is revealed the day a hero earns it. See Mastery — Levels 6 to 9.

Cross-training

A character can hold levels in any mix of the fifteen skills; a skill a character has no levels in provides none of its effects (a character with no Druid cannot lead non-native or wild units, a character with no levels in a magic school cannot cast its spells, and so on). Levels 1–5 can be trained by the Practice Skill order, so a character can be developed across several skills over successive turns.

Minor TitlesSeparate from the thirteen Imperial Titles are twenty Minor Titles — highest Melee, highest Archery, largest army, most magic items, and so on. Each is re-awarded every turn to the single leading living character and grants that character a small personal perk. Minor Titles do not count toward the Reckoning. Several of them change a skill in a particular situation only — The Strategist commands at +1 Tactical in army battles, The Duelist strikes at +1 Melee in personal combat, The Swift and The Sea-Wolf each add a movement point. Your character card lists every one of these beneath the skill it affects, with a marker saying when it applies.

A skill at zero gives nothing at all, and several of them gate an order outright: no Sage, no Research; no Thief, no Steal; no levels in a school, no spells from it; no Druid, no leading another race or any wild thing; no Necromancy, and your skeletons crumble at the end of the turn. One level in a skill is often worth far more than a sixth level in one you already have.

Eight of the thirteen Imperial Titles stand on a skill, so what you train is also what you will be judged on at the Reckoning: Tactical feeds The Grand Marshal, Naval The Sea Lord, Sage The Loremaster, all five magic schools The Archmage, White The Hierophant, Necromancy The Deathpriest, Spy The Spymaster and Explorer The Cartographer. Those count your level all the way to 9, and count equipped item bonuses with it — and each is then decided by deeds on top of the skill (battles won, discoveries made, ground mapped, spies run). The other five are pure deed or pure holding — the Throne, the Vaultkeeper, the Monster-Bane, the Beastlord and the Wildwarden — see How to Win.

The Spy skill also governs what you see of your enemies, what they see of you, and whether a hunted hero can be run down — all of that is covered under Sight, Fog & Intelligence and the Encounter order.

Every Order Explained

Each character can take one action per phase. These are the orders you'll use most.

Movement & positioning

  • Move — travel to a single adjacent province. One Move order always advances you exactly one province, no matter how many movement points you have; points instead govern how many such steps you can make across the whole turn. Moving into a defended enemy or neutral province triggers a battle. Bringing armies lets you conquer.
  • Defend — keep watch for the rest of the turn over the province you stand in and every neighbouring province your realm owns. If an enemy army marches into one of those neighbours — or an enemy hero raises, summons or seizes troops inside one — your general marches there at once and is standing in the line before the enemy can take it, so he fights as the defender, with the garrison at his side. A character with no army lies in ambush instead. The whole rule, with worked examples, is set out just below: The Defend order in full.

The Defend order in full

Defend is the order that lets one general cover more ground than he can stand on. It needs no target. From the phase it is given, the character keeps a watch that lasts until the end of the turn, and the rest of this section is simply what that watch does.

The watch

  • What is watched. The province the character is standing in, plus every province adjacent to it that your realm owns. Neutral ground, an ally's land and enemy land are never part of a watch — a general on Defend is a shield for your own provinces, not a way to attack.
  • When it starts. At the very start of the phase the order is given in — before anybody moves. A Defend written for phase 3 answers a phase-3 invasion. It then runs through every later phase of the turn without being ordered again. A phase you leave blank is a Defend order, so an idle general is always on watch.
  • The watch travels with him. The watch is always centred on wherever the general stands now. If he rides out to a neighbour he stays there afterwards — he does not march home — and from the next phase he is watching that province and its neighbours.
  • What ends it. The end of the turn; a Move or Encounter Character order in a later phase (the watch is dropped at the end of the phase before it); or losing a battle — every general on the beaten side has his watch broken for the rest of the year, unless you had already written him a fresh Defend order for a later phase, which sets a new one. Any other order (Recruit, Practice, Cast Spell, Rest…) leaves a standing watch in place: a general can Defend in phase 1, Recruit in phase 2 and still ride out in phase 2 or 3.

What makes a general ride out

Only a character leading at least one army unit rides out (a lone character ambushes instead — see below). Two things trigger him, and both put him in the threatened province before the enemy can take it:

  1. An enemy army marching in. At the start of each phase every Move is announced. If a realm you are at war with is about to march an army into a watched neighbour of yours, the general marches there at the Defend step — after the phase's non-moving orders, before any Move. He is standing in the line, with the garrison, when the invader arrives. (An invader who was killed or lost his whole column during the order step is no longer a threat, and is ignored.)
  2. An enemy host appearing inside the province. An enemy hero already lurking in one of your provinces can create an army out of nothing — Skeletal Armies from its graves, an Elemental Summon, troops wrested away by Control Undead, a hired mercenary band, a Pagan rite, or any other power that puts units under his banner where he stands. The new host does not get to seize the province when it appears, even if the province was standing empty: the question is held over to the Defend step of that same phase, when the watching general marches in and the new host must beat him to take it. (If no general's watch covers the province, nothing is held over — the host fights the garrison, or takes empty ground, on the spot.)
Defended, not retakenThis is the heart of the order. Because the general arrives first, the province never changes hands unless he is beaten, and the battle is fought with him as the defender: he gets the defender's ×1.25 edge on his own ground (×1.5 in all at your home capital, and a Dwarven host's Bulwark on top), the province's garrison fights in his line, and any other commander of yours already there joins the same host under whichever of them is the best commander. The enemy is the attacker, with none of that.

What the ride-out costs, and its limits

  • One movement point — a land point for a land march, a sea point if the move leaves or enters a sea province. A general with no points of the right kind left cannot ride out; he still defends the province he stands in. A slow column (a garrison stack, Mountain Guns) usually has only one or two ride-outs in it per turn.
  • One reaction per phase. A general who has ridden out this phase does not ride again until the next.
  • His own gate comes first. If the province he is standing in is itself about to be invaded, he holds it and rides nowhere that phase — not even to a host raised next door. That decision is his reaction for the phase.
  • Several threats at once. He answers the strongest marching column within his watch. He rides out however badly he is outnumbered — forcing a battle to slow an enemy down is a legitimate use of the order — so do not leave a weak general on Defend beside a province you would rather lose cheaply.
  • Several generals. Every general on Defend whose watch covers the province rides in, and they fight as one host under the best commander among them.
  • Ground he cannot cross. A land column cannot ride out into a sea province, a fleet cannot ride inland, and a land host stranded on flooded ground stays where it is.
  • Locked in a battle. A general whose own province still holds an unfinished battle must see it through; Defend is not a way out of a fight.
  • War only. Troops raised in your province by a realm you are not at war with cannot seize it and do not provoke a ride-out; you are simply told they are there.
  • War fronts count too. An enemy army that was already camped in one of your provinces when the turn began (see War fronts) is treated like a host raised there: when it turns on the province at the end of phase 1, a general whose watch covers that province rides in first and defends it.
  • It is a march like any other. It may raise the March skill, and if the general is beaten he falls back toward the province he rode out from.

Worked examples

In all of these your realm is Belmarch, at war with Dunhold. You own Ashfen and its neighbours Stonedown and Redgrove. Your general Thora stands in Ashfen with six units and has three land movement points.

Example 1 — an army marches in next door

Thora is given Defend in phase 1. In phase 2 Dunhold's general Korg, with five units, is ordered to Move into Stonedown, which holds a garrison of one.

  1. Start of phase 2: Korg's march is spotted. Once the phase's non-moving orders are done, Thora breaks camp and marches to Stonedown (2 points left).
  2. Korg's Move resolves. He finds Thora's six units and the garrison drawn up — seven units defending at ×1.25.
  3. If Thora wins, Stonedown never left your hands. She stays in Stonedown; from phase 3 her watch covers Stonedown and its neighbours.

Without the Defend, Korg would have fought one garrison unit and taken the province.

Example 2 — skeletons raised in an empty province

Redgrove has no garrison, but an old battlefield lies there. Dunhold's necromancer Yarrow slipped into Redgrove last turn, alone and unseen. In phase 1 Thora is on Defend in Ashfen; Yarrow casts Skeletal Armies.

  1. The cast succeeds: an Elite skeletal unit claws up under Yarrow's banner.
  2. The new host cannot take Redgrove yet. At the Defend step of that same phase Thora marches into Redgrove (one movement point) and draws up to defend it. Redgrove is still yours.
  3. Yarrow's new host must now attack her: one skeletal unit against six, with Thora holding the defender's edge. The battle is fought in that same phase.
  4. Thora wins; the skeletons crumble. Redgrove never changed hands, and Yarrow — beaten, with no friendly province beside him — is in serious trouble.

Without a general on Defend next door, an empty Redgrove would have fallen to Yarrow the moment the dead rose.

Example 3 — an elemental summoned into a garrisoned province

Same as Example 2, but Stonedown holds a garrison of two and the intruder casts Elemental Summon there.

  1. The elemental appears. At the Defend step Thora marches into Stonedown.
  2. The garrison rallies to her: eight units defend against one Veteran elemental.
  3. Had Thora not been on Defend, the elemental would have fought the two garrison units alone — and an elemental is quite capable of winning that.

Example 4 — her own province is attacked in the same phase

In phase 2 Korg marches on Ashfen, where Thora stands, while Yarrow raises skeletons in empty Redgrove.

  1. Start of phase: Korg's march on Ashfen is spotted. Yarrow's skeletons rise in Redgrove during the order step.
  2. The Defend step: Thora holds her own gate — that is her reaction for the phase. She does not ride out, and with nobody there to contest it Redgrove falls to Yarrow.
  3. Korg arrives at Ashfen and fights Thora, defending.
  4. In phase 3, if Thora won and her watch still stands, Redgrove is now enemy ground and outside her watch — winning it back takes a Move order.

One general cannot be in two places. A coordinated feint and strike is the answer to a Defend order; a second general, or a garrison in Redgrove, is the answer to that.

Example 5 — two threats, one reaction

In phase 2 Korg (five units) marches on Stonedown and a second Dunhold column of two units marches on Redgrove. In phase 3 Yarrow raises skeletons in Redgrove.

  1. Start of phase 2: Thora answers the stronger column and marches to Stonedown. The two-unit column takes Redgrove unopposed by her.
  2. Phase 3: Thora now stands in Stonedown. Whether she can answer anything in Redgrove depends on whether Redgrove borders Stonedown and is still yours — it is not, so she does not.

One ride-out per phase, always to the heaviest marching threat, and the watch moves with the general.

Example 6 — a slow column runs out of road

Instead of Thora, Ashfen is held by a hero leading four garrison units (move 1, no March skill): one land movement point for the whole turn.

  1. Phase 1: skeletons are raised in Redgrove. He marches in and defends it. His pool is now empty.
  2. Phase 3: an army marches on Ashfen, which he has left behind. He cannot ride back — no movement point remains — and Ashfen is defended only by whatever garrison you left there.

Defend is strongest on a general with movement to spare. The March skill, and a column without slow units, buy you more ride-outs in a turn.

Example 7 — Defend ordered late, and Defend interrupted

  • Thora's orders are Recruit, Recruit, Defend, Defend, Defend. An army marching on Stonedown in phase 2 is not met — there is no watch yet. The same march in phase 3 is met: the watch begins at the very start of the phase it is ordered in.
  • Thora's orders are Defend, Practice, Practice, Move, Rest. She is on watch through phases 1, 2 and 3 (Practice does not end it). The watch is dropped at the end of phase 3 because phase 4 is a Move, and phase 5's Rest does not bring it back.
  • Thora is on watch in Ashfen and your caster Ysra, standing beside her, is ordered to cast Oratory on her in phase 2; a third hero is ordered to hand her two units the same phase. In phase 2 an enemy marches on Stonedown. Both orders are non-moving, so both resolve first: Thora's troops are lifted a quality tier, the two units join her column, and then she rides out to Stonedown with all of it — still ahead of the invader. Had Ysra's Oratory been written for phase 3 instead, Thora would already be in Stonedown and the spell would simply not be worked (at no cost).

Example 8 — the general loses

Thora rides to Stonedown with six units and is beaten by Korg's much larger host.

  1. Her surviving units fall back to Ashfen — the province she rode out from — and the garrison's survivors fall back with her.
  2. Stonedown is Korg's. Thora's watch is broken: she rides out to nothing more this year, though she still defends whatever province she is standing in if it is attacked.
  3. Exception: if you had written her a fresh Defend order for a later phase, that order sets a new watch when its phase comes.

Example 9 — what Defend will not do

  • An enemy marches into a neutral province next to Ashfen: no ride-out. The watch covers only provinces you own.
  • An enemy invades your ally's province next door: no ride-out. Send help with a Move order.
  • A realm you are at peace with raises an elemental inside Redgrove: no ride-out and no battle — the troops cannot seize the province, and you are told they are standing there.
  • A lone enemy hero with no army walks into empty Redgrove and takes it: a general does not ride out against a man on his own — only an army, marching or raised, triggers him. (A lone character of yours on Defend may ambush that hero — below.)

A lone character on Defend — the ambush

A character leading no army cannot bar a road, so Defend makes them lie in wait instead. After all orders in a phase have resolved, they may ambush one enemy character who marched this phase into the province they stand in, or into any province adjacent to it (whoever owns it), forcing a personal combat.

  • Chance to spring it: 10% + 9% per level of Spy (counted to 5 — so 10% untrained, 55% for a master), halved if the quarry is leading an army. One attempt per phase, successful or not.
  • The ambusher prefers a king, then the heaviest column, then the best swordsman. Only a king may ambush a king.
  • If the quarry is next door, the ambusher slips across to them at no movement cost and stays there afterwards.
  • It is an ambush, not a challenge: the quarry's duel stance does not let them refuse it, and a commander is pulled out of his column to fight. Both sides fight with their Encounter Plans. A sprung ambush is field experience for Spy.
  • An enemy who was already in the province when the phase began is not ambushed — only one who marched in this phase.

Example 10 — the ambush

Your spy-hero Ysra (Spy 3, no army) is on Defend in Ashfen. In phase 2 Korg marches his five units into Stonedown next door.

  1. Korg's Move and any battle for Stonedown resolve first.
  2. After all orders, Ysra's ambush is rolled: 10 + 27 = 37%, halved to 18% because Korg leads an army.
  3. On a success she crosses into Stonedown and sets upon Korg in personal combat. On a failure she watches him pass, and may try again next phase only against someone who marches in that phase.

Other orders aimed at a general on watch

A general on Defend may be gone by the end of a phase, and your orders are written before anyone knows it. The sequence is built so that this does not cost you: he never rides out until every non-moving order of the phase has resolved. A spell cast on him, a column handed to him, an item passed to him, a thief's attempt on him — friend's or enemy's — all find him where the phase began, and he then rides out carrying whatever they did to him: the Oratory on his troops, the extra units in his column, the enemy's Curse on his skills.

An order misses him only when it comes in a later phase than his ride-out (he is in another province by then, and the watch does not bring him home), or in the rare case where your own orders force it to wait for another character to march in first. What each kind of order does then:

How it appears in your Chronicle

The march and the battle are filed under the general, in the phase they happened. Where several of your commanders share a battle, the full account sits under the one who commanded and the others carry a pointer to it. A general who rode out to meet a column that never arrived (its march was refused, or it ran out of movement) is told so in his own section, so a ride-out is never followed by silence. A watch carried over from an earlier phase is not re-announced every phase.

Scouting & knowledge

  • Explore — chart the province you're in, raising your exploration of it and revealing its lairs.
  • Search Lair — investigate a discovered lair in your province, fighting its guardian for the reward. The dropdown names each lair's grade, its guardian and what kind of creature that guardian is. Lairs are found on land and at sea alike.
  • Hunting Rumours — spend the phase asking after the known lairs in your province. Tick any number of them; each costs its grade in gold (Weak 1, Moderate 2, Powerful 3, Legendary 4), and for each you may learn the guardian's weakness, its hoard, and your own odds of beating it — three separate rolls at 50% + 10% per Spy level. See Hunting Rumours.
  • Spy — gather intelligence on the province your character is standing in: its defenders, owner, lairs and the characters there (a Neutral king's Broker can buy a report on a distant province instead). Success is 20% + 20% per Spy level; it costs no gold. Spy skill makes you better at it and harder to catch.

Armies & belongings

  • Recruit Armies — raise new units in a province you hold (limited per unit type, per province, per turn — see the wage rule).
  • Hire Hero — take a wandering hero in your province into service.
  • Release Hero — dismiss a hero (never your king) from your service. They become a wandering hero again, keeping any magic items they carry, and cannot be re-hired the same turn. They must not be leading any army — transfer, garrison or disband the troops first.
  • Disband Army — dismiss units you no longer wish to pay for.
  • Leave / Transfer Army — drop units as a garrison, or hand them to another of your characters in the same province.
  • Pick Up Army — take command of the unled (garrison) units stationed in the province the character currently stands in. You may take all of them or a selected subset. It cannot take units led by another of your characters (use Leave / Transfer Army for that) or hire mercenaries (use Recruit Armies). Picking up slower units lowers the character's move rate for the rest of the turn.
  • Send Item — send one or more magic items to any other living character of yours, wherever they stand (equipped items included).

Confrontation

  • Encounter Character — name any enemy character you can see and hunt them down, chasing wherever they flee (one province per phase, one movement point per step) until you corner them. The chance to corner them each phase once you share a province is a Spy contest: 50%, +5% per level of your Spy, −5% per level of theirs, and doubled if the quarry is leading an army (a column leaves a plain trail). What happens once you have them depends on who is leading troops, and the arms are counted at the moment of the catch — a quarry who recruited earlier that phase counts as armed:
    • Both of you lead armies — a full battle, the quarry's co-located comrades unifying behind them as in any other fight.
    • You lead armies and the quarry does not — a personal duel.
    • Neither leads armies — a personal duel.
    • You lead no army and the quarry does — you must first slip through their camp to reach the commander. One roll: 60%, less 8% for every unit the quarry leads, plus 5% per level of your Spy, less 5% per level of the quarry's Tactical, held between 5% and 95%. Get through and the duel is forced before the guard can rally; be turned away by the sentries and the chase ends there — the quarry's realm is told a lone challenger came for them, but no blow is struck and the duel cannot be attempted again that turn. A quarry at the head of one unit is reachable a little better than half the time; behind five, one time in four; behind seven or more, only the 5% floor remains (before your Spy is counted).
    A king answers only a mutual challenge, and only a king may run down another king. (This order now does the work of the old Encounter and Pursue both.)
  • Steal — (needs Thief 1+) rob a character standing in your province, or the province's garrison paymaster. Choose your prize: a magic item from their pack, or 5% of their realm's treasury from their strongbox (a target carrying no magic is robbed of gold instead). A paymaster yields 1 gold per garrison unit standing there. Success is 50% + 10% per level of Thief (counted to 5), halved if the target leads troops or the paymaster is guarded. Fail and you are spotted unless you slip away (Thief × 20%) — and a spotted thief is set upon: the victim draws steel and a personal fight follows, unless their duel stance is Refuse, in which case they simply drive you off. Tip A thief who does not want that fight should put Flee at the head of their Encounter Plan.

Power & self-improvement

  • Cast Spell — work one of your school's spells (see The Spellbook).
  • Use Item — activate a magic item's power.
  • Equip Items — choose which weapon, armour, shield, cloak, helm, rings, amulet and combat items the character carries into a fight (one per slot; two rings; two combat items — potions, scrolls and devices). Unslotted items are always active.
  • Practice Skill — train a skill toward level 5 on the practice ground. Success is a die roll each turn, and the chance to gain the next level falls as the skill rises (mastery is harder): practising on your own, the chance is 25% from level 0→1, 20% 1→2, 15% 2→3, 10% 3→4 and 5% 4→5. Perseverance: every practice that fails multiplies that character's next practice chance this turn by ×1.2 (cumulative — three failures make the fourth attempt ×1.73); a success resets it, and the count starts afresh each turn. If two or more of your characters practise the same skill in the same province (the order form pre-selects a companion's skill for you), they drill together: characters at the same level gain a +50% group bonus (so those rates become 37.5% / 30% / 22.5% / 15% / 7.5%), and any character training under a more-skilled mentor present (a co-trainer who was already a higher level in that skill at the start of the phase) improves automatically — a guaranteed level. Drilling: practising Tactical also drills every land and air unit the character leads, and practising Naval drills their fleets and sea-beasts: each unit may rise one quality tier, at (Green 5% / Average 4% / Veteran 3% / Crack 2%) × the skill's level per practice, doubled for Human drillmasters. The drills still run once the skill is at 5, so a master tactician keeps sharpening the host they lead.
  • Research — available to any character with Sage 1 or more. The scholar rolls for a Sage discovery (see Sage discoveries): 5% per Sage level, capped at level 5, doubled for Neutral realms, and improved by certain discoveries. When giving the order you choose a field of study — one of the categories your realm can research, or any field — and a success yields a discovery from that field; a field your realm has exhausted is shown as such and cannot be chosen. Research is the only way to make discoveries — practising Sage merely trains the skill. A successful discovery also counts as field experience for the Sage skill.
  • Rest — recover hit points.

Mastery — Levels 6 to 9

A skill's per-level benefit is capped at level 5. Levels 6–9 grant separate mastery perks instead: the even levels (6 and 8) are always-on passives, and the odd levels (7 and 9) grant a new order (for the ten non-magic skills) or a new spell (for the five magic schools). Each L7/L9 mastery order may be used at most once per turn per character, and some mastery abilities can be used only against a realm you are openly at war with.

Levels 6–9 are won only through field experience — fighting, casting, exploring, leading — and cannot be trained on the Practice ground. Every level past 5 counts toward the Imperial Titles.

The specific perk, order or spell each mastery level grants is not listed in this rulebook. Each one is revealed to you in play: when one of your characters reaches the level, the new ability appears on that character's sheet with its full description.

Armies, Quality & the Wage Rule

Armies are made of units, each with a base strength and a quality tier. A character can lead a stack of units; units can also be left as a province garrison.

The kinds of unit

Every race raises its own named troops, but they fall into a handful of battlefield roles. Which you bring matters as much as how many — terrain, the pre-battle archery volley and sea crossings all turn on unit type.

The dead need a master

A skeletal unit must be led by a character with at least Necromancy 1. Left with a commander who has none, or set down as a province garrison where nobody leads it at all, it crumbles to dust at the end of the turn — and you are told by name and place when it happens. A character who holds Necromancy only through a magic item still keeps the dead standing.

The check runs once, at the end of the turn, not after every phase — so you are free to march a skeletal host between two of your necromancers within a turn, and dropping it to a garrison in phase 2 to be picked up in phase 3 is a perfectly good manoeuvre. It must simply be in the right hands when the season closes.

This bites hardest on an Undead realm's Harvest the Field, which is the one way to raise the dead with no Necromancy at all. A raiser without the skill is warned at the time: hand the new unit to a character with Necromancy 1 before the turn ends, or it will not see the next season.

There is nothing below Green: a Green unit forced to demote disbands — the men simply go home, leaving no body and no grave to raise. (Skeletal units never change quality by any means.)

Race & the Druid requirement

A unit's race is the race of the province it was raised in. Any commander leads troops of their own race freely. Leading other races, and exotic Creatures, Monsters and fleets, needs the Druid skill — the more exotic the beast, the higher the level required. Fall short and that unit fights at a heavy penalty. Exemptions: skeletal units and neutral guards never suffer it, and Druidic realms lead all Creatures freely.

The wage rule

Every unit costs its listed price to recruit, and the same price again every turn it exists as its wage. Tax is collected and wages are paid in the same end-of-turn step, so a unit recruited this turn is also paid its wage this turn. Wages are owed on garrison (unled) units as well as fielded ones. Skeletal units are the exception at a flat 1 gold.

If your gold cannot cover your wages, units disband automatically until the shortfall clears, and they go in a set order: the rawest troops first — the greenest companies anywhere in the realm, garrison and field alike, chosen at random within an experience tier — then better men as the shortfall bites. Skeletal units are always last: the dead ask for nothing and are the final thing a bankrupt necromancer loses. If disbanding every unit is still not enough, heroes then desert.

How many you may raise in a turn

Each province caps recruiting per unit type, per turn. The cap is one of each type, with three exceptions: garrison units (two), humanoid fleets — Galleon Fleet, Elven Galleys, Dwarf Ironclads, Orcish Longships — which a shipyard lays down two of a season, and your home capital, which turns out two of anything. Mercenary bands roaming a province are hired through the same order and are not capped by type.

Movement & Marching

Getting your forces to the right province at the right phase wins as many campaigns as the fighting does. Every character carries two separate pools of movement points each turn — one for land, one for sea — and each Move order spends one point to step into a single adjacent province.

The golden rule: one Move = one province

A single Move order always advances a character exactly one province, into a tile adjacent to where they stand, no matter how large their movement pool is. What the pool decides is how many Move orders you can afford across the whole turn — and since a turn has only five phases (one order each), five provinces is the hard ceiling on how far anything can travel in a turn, however fast it is. A big pool doesn't let you leap; it lets you keep moving phase after phase without running dry.

Naming the destination — and coming back

A Move order names an actual province, not a compass heading. That matters because orders are written before the turn is fought: a character can be thrown out of a province by a lost battle, routed by Terror, or otherwise displaced between one phase and the next. Because the order names the ground you chose, a displaced character still heads for it — marching straight in if it is still adjacent, or taking one step along the shortest passable route toward it if the displacement carried them out of reach.

A displaced column takes the short road, whoever owns itWhen a displaced character has to find its own way back, it goes the shortest way. If two routes are the same length it prefers the one over friendly or neutral ground — but it will not take a longer road to avoid trouble, so a column can turn up on a rival's frontier (and, if it leads armies and you are at war, march straight in and give battle) simply because that was the quickest line to the province you named. Where that is not what you want, name a nearer province instead.

The dropdown also offers the province the character is standing in (“Stay in …”). Choose it when a later phase should hold ground, or — more usefully — to march back into a province you expected to be holding: if a battle throws you out in phase two, a phase-three order naming that province walks you straight back in. If you were never displaced, the order is simply moot: nothing happens and no movement point is spent.

How big is your pool?

A character's land pool is set by the slowest land unit they lead — a column marches at the pace of its slowest wagon — improved by that leader's March skill. A character leading no armies at all travels light and gets a flat, generous 5 land and 5 sea points (a lone rider or a hero taking ship's passage moves freely). Every unit has a base land Move rating (shown in the army roster); the common values are:

Sea movement & the Naval skill

Sea points are a separate pool, spent only when a move touches the water — sailing into a sea province, or making a coastal landing from sea onto an adjacent shore. Both cost one sea point. A fleet's sea pool is set by its slowest ship, improved by the leader's Naval skill on the very same ladder as March (+0/+1/+1/+2/+3 for slow hulls of base ≤2, +1/+1/+2/+2/+2 for faster ones). A lone hero with no ships still gets the flat 5 sea points of light travel — but Naval buys them nothing, because Naval is the command of a fleet, not of a rowing boat you're a passenger on.

Ships and sea-beasts can't walkA fleet or a primal sea-beast (Sea Giant, Kraken, Leviathan) cannot cross land. From a land province a naval force may only sail to an adjacent sea province; it reaches another coast by sailing through the sea, never by hopping shore-to-shore. A mixed land-and-sea stack likewise can't march overland while it still leads ships — split the troops off first (Leave/Transfer Army), or sail the fleet to open water.

Carrying armies across the sea

Six unit types can ferry land troops across the sea, and capacity is counted in hulls. Five are humanoid fleets — Galleon, Corsair, Elven Galleys, Dwarf Ironclads and Orcish Longships. The sixth is the Whale Cohort: although it is a wild sea-creature led by Druids rather than a humanoid fleet, it carries land units at sea exactly as a fleet does, hull for hull, and a host may cross the water on whales alone. Nothing else can carry land troops over water:

  • Each fleet unit provides 1 hull of capacity.
  • Light foot — levies, all archers, gun-crews (Mountain Guns included), garrisons, Goblin Horde, Forest Wardens, Dwarven Guard, Orc Berserkers — take half a hull each (two per ship).
  • Everything else — cavalry, siege, walking creatures and monsters — takes a full hull each.
  • Primal sea-beasts swim under their own power and need no fleet, but they carry nothing — a Kraken can't ferry a single soldier.

Hulls and flyers pool their lift. A column does not need enough shipping for everyone: any foot soldier a flyer could carry may go by air instead of by sea. Units no flyer can lift — cavalry, siege, walking creatures and monsters — must find a berth aboard ship, and if they cannot, the column stays ashore. Everything else fills the remaining berths, and whatever is left over is carried through the air, one humanoid flyer per unit. So one ship and two griffons will put two heavy foot across the water, though the ship alone could not. The convoy crosses together at the fleet's sea speed; only a host with no ships at all flies at the flyers' own pace.

If neither the hulls nor the flyers can take up the slack, the force is turned back at the water's edge — no move, no point spent. The report names both what the shipping could carry and what the flyers could lift, so you can see which was short.

Flying — over anything, including the sea

Flyers cross every terrain, and cross open sea without a fleet. There's more: if humanoid flyers can lift every foot soldier in the column (one liftable foot unit per flyer; cavalry, siege, ships and beasts are too heavy), the whole column takes to the air — it stops marching at the infantry's plodding pace and instead moves at the flyers' own speed, crossing sea freely like an all-flying host. A single grounded horse or wagon anchors everyone back to the earth.

Airborne over open waterA column carried by its flyers is a poor thing to fight with at sea. Land troops aboard a fleet fight a sea battle at ×0.75; land troops dangling from griffons or wyverns, with no ship beneath them, fight at only ×0.25, and the humanoid flyers carrying them at ×0.5. Primal winged beasts (which carry nothing) and a force of flyers alone are unaffected. Use the air to cross the sea, not to fight on it.

Crossing water by magic

Two Elemental/White spells reshape the map for a turn. Part Sea turns a sea province to plains so land armies may walk across it (no fleet may pass while it holds); Flood does the reverse, turning a land province to open sea so only fleets and flyers may enter and any land army caught there is stranded. Each cancels the other. Naval mastery beyond level 5 adds further ways to shape the sea lanes, revealed in play.

Movement in battle & conquest

Moving is how you fight and how you conquer, and it all draws on the same pools:

  • Attacking costs a move. Marching into a defended enemy or neutral province is a Move like any other — it spends one point and triggers the battle on arrival. A lone hero (no army) can walk into an empty province and take it, but cannot conquer a defended one — conquest needs armies.
  • A contested province can take more than one phase. Beaten defenders don't always vanish; a fight can carry on into later phases, and each carry-on round it grows bloodier for both sides (up to ×2.5 the casualty rate). Each phase you press the assault spends another movement point if you have one — but a carry-on battle is fought whether or not points remain, so an attacker who has run dry still finishes what they started.
  • A standing Defend can march to a neighbour. A general on Defend who answers a threat to an adjacent province of yours spends a movement point (a sea point if the march touches water) to get there, and with none left he cannot ride out. The watch begins in the phase the Defend order is given (so a phase-2 Defend meets a phase-2 invasion), and it answers an enemy host raised or summoned inside a neighbouring province of yours just as it answers one that marches in — in both cases the general arrives before the enemy can take the ground and fights as its defender. A defeat ends the watch for the rest of the year. Full rule and examples: The Defend order in full.
  • Chasing spends points too. An Encounter order runs a quarry down one province per phase at one point per step, and stalls when the pool hits zero.
  • Picking up slow troops slows you. Take command of a plodding garrison mid-turn (Pick Up Army) and your column immediately moves at that slower unit's pace for the rest of the turn; drop a slow unit and you speed back up. Points already spent are never refunded or double-charged.
Reading your reachYour order forms show each character's remaining land and sea points as you plan. The practical takeaways: a slow garrison stack reaches only an adjacent province per turn (fine for a one-hop assault); a March-trained field army can string several conquests together across the phases; and flyers or a light-travelling hero can cross the map's width in a single season.

When forces meet, the battle resolves in a fixed sequence. You don't roll anything — you set your forces and orders, and the engine fights it out — but knowing the shape of it helps you plan.

What happens to a unit on the losing side

Each unit in a beaten force is judged on its own, in this order:

  1. Destruction. One roll against the band's loser unit death figure above. That figure is multiplied by ×1.35 for every carry-on round the battle has already run (to ×2.5), reduced by certain Sage discoveries your realm may have made, halved if your commander has mastered Tactical, and raised by up to +18 percentage points if the victor fielded cavalry to ride your broken companies down in open country.
  2. If it survives, a second, separate roll decides whether it loses heart or learns something. First against loser quality loss (again halved by a master tactician): the unit drops one experience tier — and a Green unit that would drop further disbands for good; the men melt back into the population, leaving no body and no grave. Failing that, against loser quality gain: even in defeat the unit may be promoted a tier (harder the better it already is — a Green unit is 1.5× as likely as an Average one, an Elite one never; doubled for Human drillmasters).
  3. Nowhere to run. If the beaten force has no friendly adjacent province to fall back on, every surviving unit takes a further 15% chance of being cut down, and the province stays contested.

Four things change that last step. Eagle Riders always fly clear. A Dwarven-Guard-heavy force locks shields and takes no flight losses (and is not demoted in retreat either). An all-skeletal Undead host feels no fear and takes none. And a Corsair-heavy force with open water beside it pulls back to the ships.

Beaten units never change sides, and never become mercenaries. The only way your troops end up as a mercenary band is if their commander is killed and there is no friendly province adjacent for the leaderless survivors to retreat into — then they scatter and can be hired by whoever reaches them first, yourself included. Skeletal units are destroyed on exactly the same roll as anyone else; they simply never promote, never demote and never disband, so steps 2 and 3 above pass them by — and when they are destroyed their bones crumble to dust, leaving no grave to raise again.

  1. Pre-battle duel. Opposing commanders may face each other in single combat first, per the old traditions — a chance to wound or kill the enemy leader before a blow is struck.
  2. Battle magic. Eligible spells cast into the battle take effect — weakening enemy units, shielding your own, turning undead, and so on.
  3. Archery. Missile units loose before the lines close, thinning the enemy front.
  4. The strength clash. Each side's total effective strength is compared — base × quality, then modified by everything that applies. Key multipliers: racial affinity (units of the owning king's race ×1.2, units led by a same-race commander ×1.2 — both stack, so a king leading their own race fights at ×1.44); racial animosity (a commander leading a rival race — Human↔Orc, Elf↔Dwarf — fights at ×0.8, i.e. −20%); Woodland Mastery (an Elven realm's own troops ×1.5 in forest/jungle); Tactical command (×1.2 army strength per level — 1 + 0.2 × level — so Tactical 5 = ×2.0, the hard cap, titles and items included; land battles only); Archery (no strength bonus — it sharpens the pre-battle volley instead, see the Archery skill); Naval (+20% to your fleets and sea-beasts per level, on land and at sea alike); and fanatical zeal (×1.30 when it fires, at your alignment's chance — Divine 50%, Good 40%, Evil 25%, Druidic 20%, Pagan 15%, Neutral 10%, Undead never rolls but its skeletons are permanently fanatical). Defenders add a ×1.25 edge on their own ground, and a home capital ×1.5 in total. On top of all that, plus terrain, Druid leadership, spells, temper and items. The front line's width is set by terrain — 6 units on plains, desert or sea, 4 in forest or jungle, 3 in mountains — and a host deeper than its front forms up in four lines of that width: the front fights at full strength, the second at ×0.5, the third at ×0.25, the fourth at ×0.125, and anything left over beyond the fourth line also fights at ×0.125. Flyers always fight from the front and never take a line slot. On tight ground, then, sheer numbers count for much less.
  5. The fortune of battle. The clash isn't purely arithmetic: after every multiplier is applied, fortune deals one swing to the clash, and the higher fortune-adjusted total wins. The battle report shows both strengths untouched and then describes what luck did to the edge — e.g. "holds a 2.4× edge (85% to win). Fortune of battle: luck ran firmly with the defender (a 1-in-8 roll) and trimmed the edge to 1.3× — not enough." Luck is proportional to strength, so only the ratio of the two forces matters, and it is drawn from a fixed curve: a force twice as strong wins about 80% of its battles, three times as strong about 90%, and four times as strong always. Smaller edges are far less decisive — 1.5× wins about two fights in three, 1.2× barely more than half — so upsets against a modest advantage are a real part of war, while a crushing superiority is never overturned. The fortune-adjusted margin also sets the casualty band, so an upset costs the loser accordingly.
  6. Margin & consequences. How decisive the win was is the winner's lead as a share of both sides' combined strength; the six bands in the table above set each side's unit deaths and quality changes. A beaten force with an open line of retreat falls back to a friendly province; one with nowhere to run takes an extra 15% per unit "no escape" toll and the province stays contested. A fight that carries on from phase to phase grows steadily bloodier — casualty rates climb +35% per carry-on round, up to ×2.5 — so a grinding siege is far deadlier than a clean win.

Defenders fight with an edge on their own ground. Home ground belongs only to the realm that founded a capital: defending its own capital it is tougher still (×1.5 in all), and a king fighting to win back a captured capital attacks at ×1.5 — but an invader holding someone else's capital gets no such bonus. Quality is the single biggest lever: the tier multiplier runs Green ×1, Average ×2 (a fresh recruit), Veteran ×3, Crack ×4, Elite ×5 — a Veteran is worth one-and-a-half fresh recruits, an Elite two-and-a-half. Taking a contested province often takes more than one phase, because beaten defenders can stay and keep contesting it.

Every battle counts toward a TitleWhoever commands the winning side earns +1 point of battle merit for every enemy unit destroyed, and the losing commander −1 for every one of their own units lost. Merit won on land counts toward The Grand Marshal; merit won in a sea province counts toward The Sea Lord. It makes no difference whether you fought a rival realm, a neutral province's chieftain and his guard, or an enemy realm's bare garrison — a battle is a battle. A neutral guard has no commander of its own to punish, so only your own losses ever count against you there.

Personal Combat & Temper

When two characters fight — in a duel, an encounter, or a monster lair — it's resolved as personal combat: a series of rounds of archery and melee, shaped by each fighter's Melee and Archery skills, hit points, items, spells and, above all, their temper.

The odds, exactly

Every round opens at range with archery, then closes to melee. The chances are:

  • Melee to-hit is 30% + 10% per level of your own Melee to level 5, then +5% per level beyond it — so Melee 5 is 80%, Melee 6 is 85%, Melee 7 is 90%, Melee 8 is 95% and Melee 9 is 100%. The defender's skill does not reduce it. That real figure is then adjusted by temper — your attack bonus (Berserk +18, Brave +12, Cautious 0, Cowardly −4) minus the defender's guard (Cautious +14, Cowardly +6, Brave +4, Berserk −10) — and by spells, items and wards (penalties on you, auras, focus bonuses, the defender's Invisibility or dodge). Only the final result is capped, at 80%, and no attack of any kind ever exceeds it. A defender's temper guard lowers that ceiling as well as the chance, so however skilled the attacker, the most anyone can reach in melee is 80% against a Berserk, 76% against a Brave, 74% against a Cowardly and 66% against a Cautious — a trained stance is always worth something. Nothing else moves the ceiling: a guardian's evasion, a dodge ward and Invisibility all come off the real figure instead, which is precisely why mastery beats them. This is why mastery past level 5 is worth having: a Melee 6 hero facing a guardian that is −15% to hit lands at 70% (85 − 15), and a Melee 8 hero at the full 80% (95 − 15, capped), where a Melee 5 hero lands at 65% (80 − 15). The floor is 5%, so no blow is ever certain either way.
  • Archery to-hit follows the same ladder: 30% + 10% per Archery level to 5, then +5% per level beyond, plus your temper's archery bonus (Cowardly +22, Brave +8) and any penalties, auras, dodge or Invisibility on the target — the result capped at 80%. A defender's temper guard does not turn arrows, which is why a Cowardly hero makes a superb archer.
  • Every chance you are shown is a whole number, rounded down, and a d100 roll equal to or below it succeeds — so a roll that matches the shown chance always hits.
  • Melee damage is a base of 2–5, plus half your Melee level (rounded up), plus the difference if you out-skill your foe, plus your temper's damage bonus (Berserk +3, Brave +1) — less what a guarded foe shrugs off (Cautious 2). A dwarf's Mithril turns aside 2 of every blow or arrow.
  • Arrows do 2–5 plus half the archer's Archery level (rounded up). A fight that is still undecided after 20 rounds ends with both combatants withdrawing.
  • Range. Every fight opens at spell range, and a bow is only useful from bow range. A Fire action from spell range first needs a speed check to move up — 60% for a character — and from melee it needs a break-away — 40%, ±15% for each point of speed between you, then your temper's break-out modifier. A failed check spends the whole action with no shot. Every level of Archery adds +5% to both checks (fieldcraft). A foe advances one step of range per action, a fast guardian two, so most opponents are on you within a round or two; a Cowardly hero's first move to bow range simply succeeds.
  • The parting shot. When a foe steps from bow range into melee against a character who has a bow and is standing at bow range, that character looses one arrow first — before the foe's blows begin. It is a perk of the Archery skill, so a creature that merely spits or throws does not get it, and a stunned or Spiritwrack-bound archer cannot take it.
  • Fleeing succeeds at 30% + your temper's flee modifier: Berserk 10%, Brave 40%, Cautious 50%, Cowardly 60% — +25% against a slow guardian, −15% against a fast one, before bonuses like Invisibility (+30) or Charm (doubles it). A failed escape gives your foe a free strike.

The four special traits.

  • Berserk — Rage. +3 damage on every melee hit — by far the hardest hitter in the game — and shrugs off 12% of every incoming melee or archery hit (damage taken ×0.88, rounded). That is their only defence: rage carries no guard, no soak and no second wind, and their open stance hands every attacker +10%. They kill fastest and they die fastest.
  • Brave — Second Wind. +1 damage on every melee hit, and once per fight, the first time they drop below 30% of max HP, they recover 26% of their max HP on the spot — often the difference between a fight lost and a fight won.
  • Cautious — Guarded & Riposte. Every incoming melee hit is reduced by 2, and when a melee attack on them misses they have a 40% chance to answer it immediately with a free melee blow of their own. They have no attack bonus at all, so the riposte is how a Cautious fighter actually kills: they win by making the other side swing and miss. The hardest temper to kill, and the worst possible opponent for a wild attacker.
  • Cowardly — Opening Shot & Beg for Mercy. The first time a Cowardly fighter gives ground to firing range in a combat, the move simply succeeds — no roll — and they loose a shot before anything can close on them. With +22% archery to hit on top, the bow is where they do their damage. And when felled, they may plead for their life: spared 75% of the time by a Good, Divine or Druidic foe, 50% by Neutral or Pagan, 20% by Evil or Undead. If spared they escape with 1 HP, lose all remaining actions this turn, slink home by next turn, and hand the victor one random magic item. Never against a lair guardian.

Berserk hits hardest and dies most — all offence, no guard. Brave is the complete soldier, and the only temper that can come back from the brink. Cautious is the hardest to kill and punishes anyone who swings wildly. Cowardly is a poor swordsman but the best archer, thief and spy — and the safest temper for a king you cannot afford to lose.

Three rules that apply to every attack

  • No attack can be reduced below half. Guards, soaks, armour, hides, mithril and a guardian's passives all reduce damage, and they stack — but however many of them apply, a blow, arrow or spell that lands always deals at least half of what it would have dealt unreduced. Outright prevention is different and still absolute: a mirror image, a first-hit cuirass or a shield block stops an attack completely, and a hard cap ward (which limits any single hit to a share of the bearer's health) is still the last word.
  • No guardian's blow takes more than a third of your maximum HP. Whatever the creature and whatever it rolls, a single attack by a lair guardian against a character is capped at a third of that character's maximum hit points. It applies only to a guardian's blows — never to your own, and never in a fight between two characters. See Lairs, Monsters & Treasure.
  • Nothing lingers past three rounds. Every bleed, burn, poison and festering wound — a guardian's sting, a Fireball, an impaling arrow — runs for at most three rounds, however it was inflicted. A to-hit penalty burns off at least a third of itself each round (minimum 5%), so even a Legendary dragon's −30% is gone by the third round. Nothing but a combat item whose own text says so lasts a whole fight.
  • What clings to a blow bites from the next round. When an attack does damage and leaves something clinging — a blow that dazes, an arrow that blinds, a bane that sets a wound festering — the damage lands at once and the clinging part takes hold from the following round. A fighter struck this way still acts in the round they were hit. An effect that is only a duration, with no blow behind it — Spiritwrack's binding, a Psychic Blast, a raised Holy Symbol, a scroll that does nothing but hold the foe — grips immediately.

Your encounter plan

Going into a personal fight you can set a plan — which combat spells to ready, and when to try to flee or to steal. A few things aren't yours to choose: every duel opens at spell range before the fighters close to melee, and a hero always carries all of their magic items into the fight — there is no leaving them behind. Potions, scrolls and other combat-use items (healing draughts, fire scrolls, wards, banes…) are used once per fight, never consumed, and each use takes the character's action for that round. They fire on their own, when it makes sense — a healing potion only once the drinker is badly hurt, holy water only against undead or fiends, a ward once the first blow has landed — so you need not plan them. Some items are bound to a race or an alignment (marked in their description) and are inert in any other hand; they can still be sent to a character who can use them. Even so, a well-judged plan wins fights a raw stat comparison would lose.

Duels can be lethalPersonal combat is not a bloodless contest of points — a character who is beaten badly enough can be slain outright, king or hero. This is why temper and hit points matter so much, why a Cowardly king is worth their caution, and why armour that turns a blow — a dwarf's mithril (−2 to every melee hit against the wearer), a healing item, a defensive spell — can be the difference between a wound and a grave.

Magic: The Five Schools

Magic is divided into five schools, each its own skill. Level 1 in a school grants all five of its core spells; your level then sets how often each cast succeeds and how many you may cast per turn.

✨ White

Healing, blessing and holiness — resurrection, prosperity, and bane against the undead.

🧠 Psychic

The mind — charm, oratory, clairvoyance, and blasts that shatter skill and will.

🎭 Illusory

Deception — false terrain, phantom armies, hidden lairs, invisibility and mirror-selves.

🔥 Elemental

Destruction — lightning, fire, earthquake, flood, and summoned elementals.

💀 Necromancy

Death — commanding undead, raising skeletons from graves, curses and plague.

How spells are cast

Spells come in three flavours by when they work:

  • Action spells are cast as a phase order, targeting a character or province.
  • Combat spells are chosen in a personal-combat encounter plan and fire during a duel or lair fight.
  • Battle-magic spells are cast into a full army battle to swing it. Certain combat spells double as battle-magic, weakening whole enemy contingents.

Your chance to cast

Every cast is a roll. Your chance of success is 30% + 10% per level in that school, capped at 95% — so level 1 casts at 40%, level 3 at 60%, level 5 at 80% — the same whether the spell is an action, a battle-magic cast before an army clash, or a spell hurled in personal combat. Bonuses stack on top (still under the 95% ceiling): some Sage discoveries add to it; mastery of a school beyond level 5 brings further casting bonuses you discover in play. The Wailing Phylactery relic makes your first Necromancy spell each turn succeed automatically.

How many you may cast

How many spells of a school you can work depends on your level in it:

  • Action spells (per school, per turn): 1 at levels 1–2, 2 at level 3, 3 at levels 4–5. (The Warlock minor title adds one to your best school; certain Sage discoveries can ease this further.)
  • Combat spells (per school, per fight): 1 at level 1, 2 at level 2 and above — and any one spell may be cast once per fight.
Training past 5 buys a better caster, not a bigger spellA spell's effect is worked out from your school level counted to 5. Levels 6–9 make you more likely to succeed and unlock the mastery spells — they do not make a Fireball burn hotter. The spell descriptions below therefore quote figures up to level 5 and stop there.

Battlefield graves are left where armies have fallen; Necromancy draws its skeletons and control from these.

A necromancer feels the dead underfoot. Any character with at least one level of Necromancy senses whether battlefield graves lie in a province simply by standing on it — and they read every province they set foot in during the turn, not merely the one they finish in, so a march across three provinces reads all three. The sensing gives existence only: that graves are there, never how many nor what they hold. Every province so read is filed under Graveyards Known in your report, stamped with the turn it was learned, because nothing refreshes it afterwards — the graves may since have been raised or rotted away.

Magic Items & Discoveries

Magic items

Items are won from lairs, looted from the fallen, and stolen. They come in tiers, the rarest being the world's twenty-two Legendary relics. A hero can carry several, and pass them to another character with Send Item. Broadly, items:

Wearing and wieldingWeapons, armour (Mithril included), shields, cloaks, helms/crowns, rings, amulets and combat items occupy slots — one item per slot (two rings, two combat items) may be equipped at a time, and only equipped slot items give their power. Each potion, scroll or device exists once in the world, must be equipped in one of your two Combat Item slots to be carried into a fight, and fires once per fight when the moment calls for it — anything left in the pack does nothing. A character auto-equips the first item held for each slot; the Equip Items order lets you choose exactly what is worn in every slot. Everything else you carry (horns, banners, ledgers, tomes, relics…) is always active. An unequipped slot item gives nothing — not its skill levels, not its battle riders, not its temper. Equipped items can still be sent to other characters.
  • Boost a skill — adding levels of Melee, a magic school, Naval and so on (these levels even count toward the Titles).
  • Raise health, or heal.
  • Strengthen the armies a hero leads, or ease their movement.
  • Enrich your economy — extra tax, or gold per province.
  • Legendary specials — unique, powerful effects, and each relic held at the Reckoning is worth a victory point.
  • Spell items — a family of charms, rods and reliquaries, two for every core action spell. Any character holding one may order Use Item to cast that spell at a flat 95%, with no skill in the school and no casting slot spent (it works as at least a level-3 casting). No item casts a mastery spell — those come only from earning the skill.
  • Temper items — eight worn items (two for each temper) that, while equipped, change the wearer's temper in all personal combat, duels and encounters.
How far a skill item can carry you

An item that grants skill levels adds to whatever training you already have, but the benefit of most skills stops at 5 — so a Tactical 5 general who finds a +2 tome still commands at Tactical 5, and the same holds for Naval, Druid, Sage, Spy, Thief, Explorer, March and every magic school.

Seven skills are the exception — Melee, Archery and the five magic schools, because levels past 5 genuinely do more there. For those, item and aura bonuses stack on top of your trained 5 to the ceiling of 9 — which is why a Legendary blade is felt in full by a master swordsman instead of being wasted on him. For a magic school the gain is cast chance only: spell effects, and the number of spells you may work, still read your level counted to 5.

Your character card shows every point and where it came from, coloured by its source — items in gold, an aura in amber, titles in violet, Sage discoveries in blue, a Curse in red — with a marker beside any bonus that applies only in personal combat, only in army battles, only to movement, or only to that skill's own order roll.

Every item is one of a kind: the world holds at most a single copy of each, so no two heroes will ever bear the same blade. A few relics are never found in lairs at all but granted — chief among them a dwarf realm's Mithril Armour, worn by every dwarven character as their birthright.

Sage discoveries

The Sage skill unlocks the Research order (Sage 1+), the only way to make realm-wide discoveries — permanent upgrades that apply to your whole realm, in military, economy, espionage, scholarship and more (better recruit quality, richer provinces, stronger spies, and so on). They compound over the whole game, and the earliest ones you claim are the most valuable, so a little Sage from turn one pays for itself many times over. Rival spies can steal discoveries you haven't protected.

An unlooked-for findA Research order can turn up something from a field you were never studying and cannot ask for. Beyond the discoveries open to every realm, there are race-specific and alignment-specific discoveries: only a realm whose king is of that race, or of that alignment, can ever research them — and a rival's spies cannot carry off a secret their own realm could not use.

Part Seven · Gold

The Economy

Income

Your gold comes chiefly from the tax of the provinces you hold, plus bonuses from items, discoveries, titles and (for Neutral realms) trade. Each province pays its tax value in gold per turn — roughly 1–3 for a poor province, 4–6 for a prosperous one, 7–10 for a rich one (owned sea provinces pay a modest 0–2). Provinces conquered during a turn pay you at the end of that same turn. Neutral realms add a +20% trade bonus (rounded down) on top of their tax; Sage discoveries and Explorer finds can permanently raise a province's tax base, up to a ceiling of 10; and Bless / Consecrate / titles add further gold.

Expenditure

You spend on recruiting new units, wages for every unit and hired hero you keep, hiring wandering heroes, and the odd purchased action (Harvest the Field and a Broker's bought report cost 2g, Awaken the Wild costs the unit's price +2g, etc.). By far the biggest recurring cost is wages — a unit's wage each turn equals its recruit price (typically 2–4g; skeletal units are the 1g exception). A hero's per-turn wage is set by their total skill (total skill levels ÷ 4, rounded up, minimum 1g) and is recalculated whenever a skill rises; a wandering hero's opening wage is instead the alignment-distance rate of 2–4g (see Hero wages). Kings draw no wage. A commander with deep Druid mastery can bring the cost of Creature and Monster units down sharply.

Order of operations — why it matters

At turn's end, tax and wages settle together. Because a newly recruited unit is paid its wage the same turn you raise it, the first season of any unit costs double. Plan your recruiting against the income you'll actually have after that double hit — including any tax from provinces you expect to take this turn. A shipyard province can now lay down two hulls in a season, which is a fast navy and a fast way to bankrupt yourself; price both wages before you order both.

BankruptcyIf you can't cover wages, units disband — the least experienced first, anywhere in the realm, with skeletal units always last — and then heroes desert. Always keep your wage bill comfortably below your income, and disband deliberately rather than letting the shortfall choose for you.

Part Eight · Faith & Blood

The Seven Alignments

Alignment sets your battle fervour, who you can hire, and your signature action. Before each battle your force rolls for fanatical zeal; on a success it fights at ×1.30. The alignments run down the moral spectrum from holiest to unholiest — Divine, Good, Druidic, Neutral, Pagan, Evil, Undead — and that order sets hiring costs: a wandering hero costs 2g/turn +1g for every two steps between your alignment and theirs (so 2g at 0–1 steps, 3g at 2–3, 4g at 4–5, 5g at the full 6), and the two ends of the spectrum (Divine ↔ Undead) will never serve each other. Every benefit of each alignment, with its numbers:

✨ Divine — zeal 50%

  • Fanatical zeal 50% — the highest of any alignment (zeal = your force fights at ×1.30 for that battle).
  • Rest heals ×1.5: a Rest restores 30% of a character's maximum HP, and the divine blessing lifts that by half again (rounded up).
  • Bless Province adds 80% of a province's tax value (others 60%); when the King casts it the province yields +100%.
  • Divine grace: a character of yours who is slain — your king included — spends one full turn in death and returns to life at the start of the turn after that, at half health, without armies or items. The return needs holy ground: they rise in the nearest consecrated province you still hold (nearest to where the body lies), and the miracle spends that province's consecration — it is ordinary ground again, and the king may consecrate anew. If your realm holds no consecrated province when the moment comes, the grace is lost for good: the body stays where it lies (a Resurrect spell can still reach it), and one consecrated province restores only one of the fallen — the longest dead first. While a Divine king awaits his return he is not succeeded by an heir: the crown stands empty for a turn (no diplomacy or Reckoning can be declared meanwhile), then the same monarch resumes it. But a king who falls when the realm has no consecrated ground — or whose realm loses its last consecrated province while he lies dead — is succeeded by an heir like any other. A realm that loses all its land while its king lies dead is extinguished as any other.
  • Consecrate (king only): sanctify an owned province — never the home province or its neighbours, and never one bordering ground already consecrated. You may hold up to one consecrated province per level of the king's White magic. Consecrated ground gives +50% tax, defenders there roll zeal at double chance, Undead and Evil hosts attacking it fight at −15%, and it is the only ground on which your fallen can be restored (above). Holy ground that falls is holy no longer: the moment a consecrated province is captured its consecration is broken, and every unit in the conquering column has a 50% chance to gain an experience level on the spot — so a consecrated province is a prize to your enemies as well as a treasure to you. Garrison it.
  • Your king starts with White Magic +1; an heir crowned later also gains White +1.
  • Hiring: you sit at the holy end of the spectrum — dark heroes cost up to 5g/turn, and Undead heroes will never serve you.

  • Plunder: conquering a province immediately yields 50% of its tax value in gold.

Diplomacy & Alliances

Relations between realms are one of three states: Neutral, Allied (Treaty), or at War. Each turn you may submit diplomacy declarations toward each rival (the buttons sit in the Realm block at the top of your orders; your report's Diplomacy card shows the current relations), which resolve at end of turn.

  • Propose peace / alliance (Treaty). If both sides offer it, relations improve one step — from War to Neutral, or from Neutral to Allied. An overture is always reported to the realm it is sent to, whether or not they answered it, so a proposal is never made into silence — the realm courted learns of it in their Chronicle and may answer with a Treaty of their own next turn.
  • Declare War. Any one declaration of war is enough — it takes two to make peace, but only one to break it. Most realms' declarations take effect at the end of the turn, in force from the next. Evil and Undead realms keep no faith: their declaration takes effect at the start of the very turn it is lodged, so their armies may attack at once — expect no warning from them.

War fronts — when war finds armies already in place

An alliance lets armies walk through a partner's land, and a war can be declared while columns stand side by side. So a turn can open with two realms at war and an army of one already standing inside a province of the other, or with the two realms' armies sharing a province. Nobody marched, so no battle has been triggered — but the war cannot simply leave them there.

  • Phase 1 is yours to act. Nothing is fought at the start of the turn. Either side may use phase 1 to withdraw, to march in reinforcements, to attack first with a Move, or to put a general on Defend next door.
  • At the end of phase 1 — after every phase-1 order, the Defend step and all marches — any army still standing where it stood when the turn began turns on the province. It is the attacker: the owner's commanders and standing garrison defend with the defender's ×1.25 edge, a general whose Defend watch covers the province rides in first, and a province nobody defends is simply taken. If several of your columns are camped there, each attacks in turn, strongest first, until the province falls or the fight is left unfinished.
  • Two armies sharing a province neither of them owns fall on each other at the same moment. If the ground belongs to a third realm that is at war with neither, it stays that realm's whatever the result.
  • Already fought over? A province that saw a battle during phase 1 — a march into it, a host raised there — has had its fight; nothing further happens at the end of the phase, and anything left unresolved carries on at the start of phase 2 as usual.
  • You are warned. When war is declared at the end of a turn, both realms are told in their Chronicle which of their armies stand on a war front, so the next turn's orders can deal with it. (An Evil or Undead declaration takes effect before phase 1 of the turn it is made, so its armies attack at the end of that very phase 1 — no warning.)

Allies open their borders to one another and can pool forces, and — importantly — an allied bloc can jointly reach the dominance needed to Call the Reckoning. But allies do not share a victory: at the Reckoning every realm still competes for the throne. Expect late betrayals as the endgame nears. Certain aggressive mastery orders can only be used against a realm you are at war with.

Your bloc is yours alone

For the Reckoning, your bloc is you and the realms you are directly allied to — nobody else. An ally's own alliances are that ally's business and count for nothing toward your claim. If you are allied with Belmarch, and Belmarch is separately allied with Dunhold, then Dunhold is not in your bloc — you may even be at war with them.

And an alliance is never a free ride to the throne: every ally in your bloc raises the share of the world you must control before you can Call the Reckoning. See How to Win for the arithmetic.

In a game of three realms or fewer no alliance can be sealed — realms may still make peace, but the step from Neutral to Allied is barred.

Computer-run realmsAny realm played by the computer declares war on every human realm it meets, and while a human king still reigns the computer realms keep the peace with one another and seek alliance among themselves — expect them to act as one bloc against you. They hire every wandering hero they can reach, concentrate their armies into a single great host under their best general, leave only token garrisons behind, and send their spare heroes into your land as raiding casters, hero-hunters and spies.

How to Win: The Reckoning

There is no turn limit. The game ends when one power comes to dominate the world and lays claim to the vacant Imperial throne — the Reckoning — or when only one realm remains.

Conceding

A realm may concede with its orders (the control sits in the Realm block at the top of your orders, beside dispatches and diplomacy). This is permanent: from that turn its heroes only Defend and it can never rejoin. If only one un-conceded realm remains — or every realm still standing is one mutual alliance — the game ends at once: the sole survivor wins, or within an allied bloc the realm with the highest Legacy score, and the final standings are shown.

Calling the Reckoning — the three conditions

Calling the Reckoning is a king-only act. Three things must all be true:

  1. It is Turn 10 or later. The throne cannot be claimed while the realms are still settling, however fast a start someone has made.
  2. Your King is alive.
  3. You hold the bar — your realm, together with the realms you are directly allied to, controls at least your threshold share of the world's total tax base. And if allies are helping you carry it, you personally hold at least half that bar in your own right.
What counts as the world's tax baseThe threshold is measured against the entire world's tax base — the tax of every province on the map, land and sea, both the ones players hold and the neutral provinces still out there to be conquered. Since sea provinces are claimable and pay their own modest tax, the deep counts toward dominance just as the land does. Dominance therefore means truly commanding the world, not merely the slice that's been carved up so far — so early in a game, while much of the map is still neutral, the bar is genuinely hard to reach.

Working out your bar — three steps

Your portal always shows you the finished figure, but here is exactly how it is reached. Start with the base for your game's size, take off anything the long war has worn away, then add for each ally standing with you.

A worked example

A four-realm game. It is Turn 12. You are allied with one other realm.

  • Base for four realms: 45%
  • Turn 12 is before Turn 15, so nothing has worn away: still 45%
  • One ally, at 50 ÷ 4 = 13 points each: +13

Your bar is 58%. The two of you together must hold 58% of the world's tax base — and because allies are carrying it with you, you must personally hold 29% (half of 58) before you may lodge the claim.

If you were standing alone instead, your bar would be 45% and — since your own share is your bloc's share — the personal floor would never bind you.

A small ally makes your claim harder, not easierRead the arithmetic the other way and a trap appears: an ally adds their whole tax base to your bloc, but they also add the full ally step to your bar. An ally who holds less of the world than the step is a net loss. In a four-realm game that means an ally with under 13% of the world is actively costing you the throne. Alliances near the endgame should be made with powers, not with passengers.
A claim is lodged by a realm, not by a passengerAn alliance can carry the bar, but the realm that actually lodges the claim must be a power in its own right: its own tax base must be at least half the bar — 29% where the bar is 58%. An ally holding a sliver of the world cannot claim the throne on a stronger partner's strength, so it is the greatest realm in a bloc that must call it. This holds for the life of the claim, not just the moment it is made: if the claimant is later cut below half the bar while their allies still stand, the claim collapses. Striking the claimant is therefore a second way to break an allied claim, alongside shrinking the bloc. A realm that meets the full threshold alone is never affected by any of this.

The world tires of war

A game that will not end is not allowed to run forever. If no Reckoning has been called by the end of turn 15, the crown's price begins to fall: the base drops 5 percentage points at the end of that turn and at the end of every turn after it, until it reaches a floor of 25% and holds there. A six-realm game opening at 40% is therefore fought at 35% on turn 16, 30% on turn 17, 25% on turn 18 — and no lower. The ally step is added on top of whatever the base has fallen to, so an allied bloc's bar erodes at the same pace as a lone realm's. Every realm is told in the public record each time the bar falls.

The clock stops when a throne is claimedThe erosion counts only turns that pass with the throne unclaimed. The moment a Reckoning is called the bar freezes exactly where it stood, and that frozen figure is what the contesting realms must drive the claimant below during the grace period — it will not slide further in the claimant's favour while they are being fought. If the claim collapses, the erosion picks up again from where it left off rather than jumping ahead to catch up with the turn number.
Your bar is fixed when you lodge the claimThe bar is worked out once, at the moment the claim is made, and held for its whole life. Breaking off an alliance during the grace period therefore costs you your ally's lands without giving you back the ally step — there is no dropping a friend to lower your own bar and rescue a failing claim. Choose your bloc before you reach for the crown.

The grace period — two turns to contest it

Calling the Reckoning doesn't end the game at once. It's announced to everyone, and the game is set to conclude at the end of the second full turn after the call. Those two turns are the endgame:

  • Rivals get a last chance to break the claim — if the claimant (or bloc) is driven back below the bar before it resolves, the claim lapses and play continues.
  • This is when trailing realms ally against the leader, pool their armies, and try to shatter the dominant power — the classic "everyone turns on the front-runner" finish.
  • Those who can't stop it instead sprint to improve their own final standing.

The Reckoning is scored on Titles, not raw size

If dominance holds when the two turns are up, the world is judged. The winner is the realm with the greatest total Legacy, scored across the thirteen Imperial Titles and the Legendary relics. Each Title is awarded to the single leading realm (or, for character-based Titles, the realm whose character leads) — and only to a character sworn to a realm; a title with nobody to its name stands vacant. The tax-base Title (The Throne) is worth 20; every other Title is worth 10; each Legendary relic held is worth 1 — 140 points in Titles and 22 in relics on the board.

Part Nine · Strategy

Your First Three Turns

A strong opening is built on two facts. First, every unit costs double in its first season (raise cost, then a wage the same turn). Second, conquests are counted before tax is collected, so a province taken this turn pays you this turn. Together: recruit as much as your conquests will pay for, and no more.

Don't march out on turn 1 with your starting armyYour king's small starting force is not enough to take even a lightly-guarded province reliably. Attacking on a thin margin is a coin-flip at best; lose and you'll spend three turns recovering. Recruit first.

Turn 1 — recruit, explore, and blood a hero

  • Recruit at home first. Your rich home province lets you raise the most units. Build a stack that turns a coin-flip assault into a near-sure thing — but stop short of spending everything, or the wage bill will bankrupt you and your new troops will disband themselves.
  • Search your home province's guaranteed lair. Send a hero to clear the weak guardian there: free gold, a chance to improve the hero's fighting skill, and a shot at an item. This is the first step on your hero power curve — do it every turn there's a lair to clear.
  • Explore the province you mean to take next with another hero, so you can see its defenders before you commit.
  • Start cross-training. Spend a spare order to Practice a foundational skill — Sage (discoveries compound all game; the earliest are worth the most), a first level of Druid (so a leader can command mixed and captured troops), or Tactical on your main war-leader.
  • Attack in phase 3 or 4, not phase 5. Taking a defended province usually spans two phases even when you win every fight — start early enough to finish inside the turn.

Turn 2 — take the poor province first, then compound

  • Target the poor province, not the rich one. Poor provinces have the fewest defenders; your odds collapse far faster than the tax reward rises as guards get thicker. A poor tile taken safely beats a rich tile you fail to take. Save prosperous provinces for turn 3, once your stacks are bigger.
  • Concentrate — don't split. One strong stack taking provinces in sequence beats two weak stacks attacking in parallel. Two 50% attacks are worse than one 90% attack followed by another.
  • Now you hold two provinces, so your recruiting capacity has doubled. Raise garrisons in both, still keeping wages well under income.
  • Keep clearing lairs. Your lair-hunting hero should be on their second or third lair by now — noticeably tougher, and probably carrying an item.

Turn 3 — the shape of your game emerges

  • Garrison what you take. A conquered province left empty is retaken for free by a single enemy hero. Leaving a cheap garrison unit behind is the cheapest insurance in the game — and a general left on Defend in the middle of your lands covers every province around him, against armies that march in and against hosts raised by a lurking necromancer or summoner alike.
  • Now Tactical earns its keep. Once your war-stacks reach five or six units, Tactical is the best gold-free multiplier there is — it costs orders, not coin. Keep training it on your main leader, and remember it is also the standing measure of The Grand Marshal.
  • Keep cross-training the rest. A pinch of Explorer on scouts (faster charting, auto-spotting lairs), Spy on an intelligence hero, a magic school on a caster. Rounded heroes give you more good options every single turn, and every skill you push past 5 later feeds an Imperial Title.
  • Pick a direction. Look at where the neutral wild country and the other players are. Expanding toward a neighbour invites war before you're ready; expanding into the wild buys time, though primal guardians are tougher.
  • Watch the wage line every turn. If wages ever creep above income, disband something on purpose before the engine disbands it for you.

Ten things new players get wrong

  1. Marching out on turn 1 with the starting army — it usually fails.
  2. Spending all their gold on turn 1 — the wage bill lands the same turn and the new troops disband.
  3. Forgetting that garrisons cost wages too, not just field armies.
  4. Splitting forces — two half-strength attacks lose to one strong attack repeated.
  5. Attacking mountains with a huge stack — the battle line is narrow, so numbers can't all fight.
  6. Marching cavalry into forest, where they fight badly.
  7. Expecting to take a province in a single phase — budget two.
  8. Leaving a conquered province empty — it's taken back for free.
  9. Ignoring lairs — the cheapest gold, and the only source of permanent hero HP and items.
  10. Sending a hero into a lair a grade above their measure — check the readiness table first; the odds fall away fast.
The habit that winsEvery turn, clear a lair, take a province you can take safely, keep wages under income, and spend one spare order improving a skill. Do that consistently and you'll be the dominant power when it's time to Call the Reckoning.
